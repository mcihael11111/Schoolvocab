## Project Proposal and Management

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: design brief, project planning, design folio, timeline management

### Definition
Project proposal formally outlines the design problem, target users, design intent and scope boundaries. Project management systematically plans and tracks progress through defined phases—establishing clear objectives, allocating resources and time, identifying potential obstacles, and maintaining focus on intended outcomes. Professional project management distinguishes successful design projects from chaotic attempts.

### Why It Matters
The HSC Major Design Project assessment explicitly requires demonstrating project management competence. Submitting a clear proposal early (typically term 1, year 12) enables timely teacher feedback guiding productive development. Examiners assess whether your folio demonstrates systematic project management—not just random work scattered across the year. Strong project management prevents last-minute panics, ensures project completion within term deadlines, and enables quality work rather than rushed final products.

### Scenario
Two students began year 12 design projects identically ambitious. Student A developed a detailed proposal including design brief, target user analysis, proposed timeline and resource requirements, then systematically monitored progress. Student B jumped directly into production without formal planning. By term 3, Student A delivered polished projects meeting their stated objectives, whilst Student B abandoned incomplete work when production exceeded unexpected complexity.

### Example
> "My project proposal (submitted week 2, term 1) outlined: design challenge addressing meal planning for busy professionals, target users (25-45-year-old working parents), success criteria (reduced weekly planning time from 90 to 30 minutes, minimised food waste through better ingredient tracking, cost under $150 for prototype), and scope boundaries (physical system prototype, not software, designed for Australian kitchen environments). This formal proposal guided all subsequent development and enabled teacher feedback preventing wasted effort on impractical directions."

## Project Development Timeline (Gantt Chart)

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: production planning, time management, critical path analysis, feasibility

### Definition
A Gantt chart visualises project timeline across four school terms, mapping task sequencing and dependencies. The critical path identifies minimum time required for project completion; recognising which tasks must complete sequentially versus which can run concurrently enables efficient scheduling. Gantt charts enable identifying if timelines are realistic or require scope reduction.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment includes project management evidence. Submitting a Gantt chart demonstrates you've seriously considered timeline feasibility. Many projects fail because students significantly underestimate production time; Gantt charts force realistic time estimation by requiring breakdown of major tasks into component activities and accounting for overlapping dependencies. This realism prevents the common scenario of incomplete HSC projects due to poor planning.

### Scenario
A student initially thought she could design and produce a piece of custom furniture within the HSC year. Creating a detailed Gantt chart (design finalisation 3 weeks, material procurement 2 weeks, fabrication 6 weeks, finishing 4 weeks, evaluation 2 weeks = 17 weeks minimum) revealed 17 weeks critically required, leaving minimal buffer in the 40-week school year. This realistic assessment enabled her to reduce scope appropriately, ensuring completion.

### Example
> "My Gantt chart mapped term-by-term progression: Term 1 (weeks 1-10): design research and development (needs analysis survey, design brief finalisation, iteration sketching), Term 2 (weeks 11-20): detailed specification development and material procurement, Term 3 (weeks 21-33): prototype fabrication (weeks 21-28 component production, weeks 29-32 assembly, weeks 31-33 finishing—with overlapping activities where possible), Term 4 (weeks 34-40): final refinement, photographic documentation, and folio preparation. Critical path extends through 3-week finishing process blocking preceding tasks' acceleration."

## Design Folio Requirements (Max 80 A4 Pages)

- type: know-it
- level: advanced
- related: design documentation, design folio, communication and design, project management

### Definition
The HSC design folio (maximum 80 A4 pages) documents your complete design process and serves as primary assessment evidence. Expected components include: Design Brief & Analysis (problem definition, target user research), Design Development (sketches, iterations, decision rationale), Technical Specifications (detailed drawings, dimensions, material selections), Production Planning (manufacturing methodology, Gantt chart, cost analysis), Prototype Documentation (construction process, challenges encountered, solutions implemented), Testing & Evaluation (evidence of prototype performance against criteria), and Reflection (honest assessment of success and learning).

### Why It Matters
The design folio accounts for 60% of HSC marks for the Major Design Project. Examiners assess whether your folio demonstrates rigorous design thinking, evidence-based decision-making, and honest evaluation. A poorly organised folio with missing components significantly impacts your marks regardless of prototype quality. Conversely, excellent folio documentation can partially compensate for prototype limitations if you clearly articulate design reasoning and acknowledge shortcomings. The folio's completeness and organisation directly determine assessment outcomes.

### Scenario
A student with a well-executed prototype submitted a 35-page folio lacking critical components—no design research, minimal sketches showing development process, insufficient technical documentation for fabrication reproducibility. Assessors couldn't award marks for evidence of research, iterative thinking or design justification because no documentation existed. The excellent prototype alone couldn't compensate for folio deficiencies, resulting in disappointing marks.

### Example
> "My 80-page design folio comprised: Design Brief & Research (pages 1-12, including detailed needs analysis survey and user persona development), Design Development (pages 13-35, documenting 30+ sketch iterations with written analysis of evolution), Technical Specifications (pages 36-55, comprehensive orthographic and isometric drawings with detailed annotations), Production Planning (pages 56-62, Gantt chart, cost breakdown, WHS analysis), Prototype Documentation (pages 63-72, photographic record of construction process, challenges and solutions), Evaluation (pages 73-78, testing documentation and user feedback integration), and Reflection (pages 79-80, honest assessment of design success against criteria)."

## Project Management Strategies

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: project planning, risk management, time management, feasibility planning

### Definition
Project management strategies encompass approaches for maintaining project momentum and handling obstacles: regular progress monitoring (tracking actual progress against planned timeline), contingency planning (identifying potential problems and mitigation strategies), scope management (resisting feature creep whilst meeting core requirements), quality standards (maintaining consistent quality throughout production), and stakeholder communication (regular feedback with teachers or project advisors).

### Why It Matters
Professional design projects fail not because of technical difficulty but due to poor management—losing focus on core objectives, underestimating timelines, or allowing perfectionism to delay completion. HSC assessment values students demonstrating management sophistication—recognising that effective project management often matters more than technical brilliance. Additionally, HSC design projects simulate professional reality; managing projects with deadlines, resource constraints and competing demands develops essential professional skills.

### Scenario
A student's design project began excellently but faltered in term 3 when she discovered a fundamental design flaw in her chosen production method. Panic ensued, prompting late nights and stressed work. Effective project management might have identified this risk earlier, enabling contingency planning. A colleague who regularly met with her teacher for feedback caught similar issues early, enabling calm redesign rather than crisis redesign.

### Example
> "I implemented project management strategies including: weekly progress monitoring against Gantt chart (tracking actual versus planned progress and identifying delays), contingency planning for key risk areas (if timber machining exceeded estimated time, I identified alternative materials acceptable for delay compensation), regular teacher consultation (fortnightly meetings reviewing progress and seeking feedback on design direction), and explicit scope boundaries (specifying core requirements versus 'nice-to-have' features enabling conscious priority if timeline pressure emerged). These strategies maintained project momentum and prevented last-minute crises."

## Prototyping and Modelling

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: design development, iterative refinement, testing and evaluation, production

### Definition
Prototyping creates physical representations of designs enabling testing, evaluation and refinement. Prototyping ranges from rough models (quick, low-cost explorations testing specific aspects) through functional prototypes (fully working versions testing complete performance) to refined prototypes (production-quality versions). Effective prototyping employs iterative cycles—build, test, learn, refine—rather than perfecting designs theoretically.

### Why It Matters
Prototyping is essential to design development. The Major Design Project assessment explicitly requires prototype development with photographic documentation. Rather than assuming theoretical designs will work, prototyping reveals real-world complications requiring solution. Many of the best design insights emerge from prototype testing. Examiners value students demonstrating iterative refinement based on prototype learning—progressing from rough prototypes through refined versions based on accumulated knowledge.

### Scenario
A student's theoretical design calculations predicted the prototype would work perfectly. Physical prototyping revealed unexpected complications: components that appeared to fit in CAD drawings interfered physically, material flexibility caused unintended deformation, and assembly sequences proved impossible as designed. Each prototype iteration revealed problems, enabling targeted solutions. The final prototype bore little resemblance to the original theoretical design, demonstrating prototyping's essential value.

### Example
> "I developed three prototype iterations: Prototype 1 (quick 3D-printed rough model testing basic proportions and component interaction, revealing fundamental assembly sequence problems), Prototype 2 (refined fabrication using final materials and methods, identifying specific joint failures and fastener inadequacies), Prototype 3 (production-quality prototype incorporating all learning, demonstrating successful functionality). This iterative approach enabled learning from each prototype, preventing expensive late discovery of problems."

## Testing and Evaluation Methods

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: quality control, specifications, performance assessment, evidence-based evaluation

### Definition
Testing systematically assesses whether prototypes meet design specifications and intended functionality. Testing methods include: performance testing (measuring functional capability), durability testing (determining longevity under normal conditions), user testing (evaluating how actual users interact with designs), and aesthetic evaluation (assessing visual appeal and finish quality). Rigorous testing provides objective evidence of design success rather than subjective opinions.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment requires evidence of rigorous prototype testing and honest evaluation. Examiners distinguish between students claiming designs work versus students demonstrating evidence they work. Testing documentation—showing measurement results, photographic evidence of tests, user feedback records—proves your claims. Additionally, testing often reveals design shortcomings; acknowledging and explaining these demonstrates maturity rather than weakness.

### Scenario
A student claimed their prototype worked perfectly but provided no testing evidence. Assessors requested documentation proving functionality claims. When forced to conduct formal testing, she discovered that under realistic load conditions, her prototype deformed slightly, joint fasteners loosened after 20 use cycles, and aesthetic finish wasn't as refined as claimed. Honest documentation of these shortcomings, with explanation of how future refinement would address them, produced more credibility than unsupported excellence claims.

### Example
> "I conducted systematic testing including: load testing (applying 10kg sustained load, measuring deformation with precision ruler—results showing <2mm deformation within specifications), durability testing (50 repetition cycle testing joint fasteners with torque gauge verifying no loosening beyond specification), and user testing (12 target users interacting with prototype, documenting ease of use on questionnaires rating difficulty 1-5 scale, and observing actual usage patterns for 20 minutes each). Testing documentation with photographs, measurement records and user feedback provided objective evidence of prototype success."

## Iterative Refinement

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: prototyping and modelling, design development, learning cycles, product improvement

### Definition
Iterative refinement is repeated cycles of designing, making, testing, learning and improving. Each iteration addresses identified limitations from previous iterations, progressively advancing toward design goals. Iteration differs from random modifications—each cycle is deliberate, learning-focused improvement rather than arbitrary changes.

### Why It Matters
The most impressive HSC design projects don't emerge perfect immediately; they improve through systematic iteration. Examiners value students demonstrating learning and adaptation. Your design folio should explicitly show how prototype testing revealed limitations and how subsequent iterations addressed these findings. This demonstrates sophisticated design thinking—recognising that initial designs rarely work perfectly and that improvement emerges through deliberate refinement.

### Scenario
A student's design development process was transparent in the folio: Prototype 1 testing revealed joint failures (shown with photographic evidence), Prototype 2 incorporated reinforced joinery addressing this (with explanation of the specific modifications), and testing of Prototype 2 revealed minor deformation under load (again documented), prompting Prototype 3 material specification upgrade and internal reinforcement. This clear iteration narrative demonstrated learning and improvement far more effectively than claiming designs were perfect initially.

### Example
> "My design refinement comprised three distinct iterations: Iteration 1 revealed adhesive-only joinery insufficient for load-bearing joints; Iteration 2 incorporated mechanical fastening with structural reinforcement (documented through sketches explaining design changes and rationale). Iteration 2 testing revealed component misalignment during assembly; Iteration 3 redesigned assembly sequence with guide features preventing misalignment. Iteration 3 user testing revealed difficulty manipulating assembly jigs; final refinement simplified user interface. This documented iteration sequence demonstrates deliberate learning-driven improvement."

## Design Justification

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: design decisions, criteria matching, specification achievement, rationale articulation

### Definition
Design justification explains why specific design choices were made, demonstrating that decisions derived from requirements and constraints rather than arbitrary preference. Justifications reference design specifications, research findings, material properties or manufacturing capabilities—grounding design choices in objective reasoning rather than subjective taste.

### Why It Matters
The HSC written examination and design folio assessment require explaining design decisions. "I liked that colour" proves nothing; "I selected this colour because research showed my target market associates it with trustworthiness and environmental consciousness" demonstrates design thinking. Examiners distinguish between students making arbitrary choices versus those whose every decision can be justified by reference to requirements or research. This distinction determines assessment bands.

### Scenario
A student's design folio included extensive aesthetic refinements (colour choices, texture decisions, proportional adjustments) but provided no justification for these decisions. Assessors questioned whether these choices derived from design intent or arbitrary preference. Revising the folio to explicitly reference how aesthetic choices served target market preferences and communicated design intent transformed weak design documentation into sophisticated design thinking demonstration.

### Example
> "I justified every significant design choice: shape derives from ergonomic research (40mm diameter grip accommodates hands from 5th to 95th percentile), material selection prioritises durability (hardwood suitable for high-use environments) and sustainability (FSC-certified source), colour choice reflects survey feedback (73% preferred natural tones over synthetic colours), surface texture improves grip security (prevents slipping with wet or sweaty hands as identified in user testing). This justification demonstrates that design decisions emerged from research and requirements, not arbitrary preference."

## Material Selection Rationale

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: material selection criteria, material properties, sustainability, design specifications

### Definition
Material selection rationale explains why specific materials were chosen by comparing alternatives against selection criteria (functional requirements, cost constraints, environmental impact, manufacturing feasibility, aesthetic properties). Effective rationale acknowledges trade-offs—no material is perfect for all criteria, so selection involves conscious prioritisation.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment includes evaluation of material selections. Rather than accepting default materials, demonstrating that you've considered alternatives and consciously selected based on identified criteria shows sophisticated design thinking. Additionally, material selection rationale appears in written examinations where you must analyse whether designers' material choices were justified by performance requirements or driven by other factors like cost-reduction.

### Scenario
A student initially selected stainless steel for outdoor storage due to corrosion resistance. Developing a detailed material selection rationale comparing stainless steel against galvanised steel and aluminium (considering functionality, cost, aesthetics, manufacturing methods, and sustainability) revealed galvanised steel as optimal—excellent corrosion resistance in coastal environments, lower cost enabling larger unit production, established fabrication methods readily available, and proven track record in similar applications. This systematic analysis produced better material choice than initial assumption.

### Example
> "I selected anodised aluminium 6061-T6 against alternatives through systematic comparison: Stainless steel offered superior corrosion resistance but excessive cost ($8/kg vs $3/kg aluminium) and manufacturing complexity; galvanised mild steel offered corrosion protection and cost-efficiency but inferior strength-to-weight ratio and difficulty controlling surface finish aesthetics; titanium offered superior strength-to-weight but costs exceeded project budget (×12 aluminium cost) and manufacturing required specialised equipment. Aluminium's strength-to-weight ratio (1.8 vs steel's 0.3), established anodising infrastructure for colour options, cost efficiency, and lightweight properties critical for portable applications justified final selection."

## Production Log Documentation

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: prototype documentation, production planning, process recording, evidence collection

### Definition
A production log is a chronological record of design development and manufacturing work, documenting dates, activities, challenges encountered, decisions made, materials used and time spent. Production logs create accountability, enable progress tracking against plans, and provide evidence of development work completed. Photographic documentation complements written logs, creating visual evidence of progress.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment requires evidence of production work completed. Production logs with dates and photographic documentation prove you've invested substantial effort. Logs also help examiners understand your process if final outcomes reveal shortcomings—they can assess whether problems resulted from insufficient effort or from legitimate design challenges. Additionally, logs prevent last-minute claims of work not actually completed; documentation creates honesty.

### Scenario
A student submitted claims of 100+ hours production work but lacked documentation. Sceptical examiners suspected exaggeration and marked accordingly. A peer who maintained a detailed production log with weekly entries, photos, and time tracking created irrefutable evidence of 120 hours actual work. When both submitted similar-quality prototypes, the peer's documented effort and transparent process received significantly higher marks than claims without evidence.

### Example
> "My production log (in design folio pages 63-72) documented: Week 1-2 (14 hours): component fabrication—timber machining and metal forming, with photographic record of process and finished components; Week 3 (16 hours): assembly and joint refinement—addressing misalignment problems identified during test assembly; Week 4 (12 hours): finishing processes—sanding progression (80, 120, 220, 320-grit) with documentation of surface defects addressed; Week 5 (8 hours): final refinement and quality inspection. Photographs at each stage documented process, component completion and quality verification, providing visual evidence of 50 hours documented production work."

## Quality Assurance

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: quality control, specifications, inspection, standards compliance

### Definition
Quality assurance involves systematic processes ensuring designs meet intended standards and specifications. QA includes documentation of quality standards (what must be achieved), inspection procedures (how compliance is verified), corrective actions (how shortcomings are addressed), and final acceptance criteria (determining when products are complete and acceptable). Quality assurance differs from quality control—QA is preventative (setting standards before production) whilst QC is corrective (finding problems after production).

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment values quality awareness. Rather than hoping prototypes work out acceptably, demonstrating systematic quality assurance shows professional design discipline. This includes: developing explicit quality standards for your prototype (what constitutes acceptable dimensions, surface finish, structural integrity), inspecting against these standards during production, and documenting any quality issues and their resolution. This demonstrates production maturity beyond simply building something that "looks okay."

### Scenario
A student completed prototype fabrication without formal quality assurance. Upon informal examination, they discovered dimensional errors (some components varied by 3mm from specification), surface finish inconsistencies (variable sanding quality across surfaces), and structural concerns (some fasteners weren't torqued to specification). Rework consumed several weeks. A peer who implemented quality assurance procedures (measuring components immediately after fabrication, sanding to specification with documented methods, torquing fasteners to documented values) discovered and addressed minor issues immediately, preventing late-stage catastrophes.

### Example
> "I implemented quality assurance procedures including: documented standards (all components must be within ±0.5mm specification, surface finish must be smooth with no visible defects, fasteners must be torqued to 3.5Nm specification), inspection schedule (measuring each component upon completion before assembly), corrective actions (refinishing surfaces failing initial inspection standards, re-machining components exceeding tolerance before assembly), and final acceptance criteria (complete prototype must pass drop test, load test, and 50-cycle durability test before declaring completion). Documentation showing which items failed inspection and corrective actions demonstrated quality discipline."

## Photographic Documentation

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design folio, evidence collection, production documentation, visual communication

### Definition
Photographic documentation visually records design development and prototype production through strategic images: design sketches and models, component fabrication at various stages, assembly sequences, finished prototype from multiple angles and in use contexts, and testing procedures. Photographs provide objective visual evidence complementing written documentation and transform design folios from text-heavy to visually engaging.

### Why It Matters
Photographs make design folios dramatically more compelling and accessible. Rather than reading descriptions of complex processes, viewers see actual work. Photography also ensures accountability—it's harder to exaggerate production effort when photographic evidence exists. The Major Design Project assessment rewards design folios including quality photography; it demonstrates professionalism and communication sophistication. Additionally, photographing prototypes in realistic use contexts enables viewers to understand functionality intuitively.

### Scenario
Two design folios described identical prototype designs but one included minimal photography (blurry overview photos) whilst the other featured strategic professional photography (components during fabrication, assembly sequence clearly documented, finished prototype from multiple angles, prototype in realistic use context). Despite identical design work, the photographically documented folio appeared far more impressive and professional, directly influencing assessment.

### Example
> "My design folio included 45 strategic photographs: 8 images documenting component fabrication (showing CNC machining, hand-finishing, material preparation), 6 images showing assembly sequence (revealing how components fit together and fastener placement), 5 images of finished prototype from different angles and lighting, 8 images showing prototype in use contexts (actual user interaction, demonstrating functionality and ergonomic fit), and 12 images documenting testing procedures (load testing apparatus, durability testing setup, user testing sessions). Professional lighting and composition made photographs clear and informative rather than incidental."

## Final Evaluation Against Criteria

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: evaluation, design specifications, success measurement, honest assessment

### Definition
Final evaluation assesses whether completed designs successfully achieved stated objectives by comparing actual outcomes against predetermined success criteria. Rigorous evaluation acknowledges both achievements and shortcomings, explaining what worked well and what future refinement might address. Honest evaluation demonstrates design maturity; pretending flawless designs rarely emerge shows sophistication more than claiming perfection.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment includes evaluation as explicit assessment criterion. Examiners expect you to honestly assess whether your prototype successfully met design specifications and solved the stated problem. Acknowledging limitations (if you're aware of them) demonstrates better understanding than claiming perfection you don't actually believe. This is particularly important in HSC written examinations where you must evaluate whether existing designs successfully solve stated problems—demonstrating this critical thinking ability matters greatly.

### Scenario
A student's design brief specified the prototype must: accommodate items from 150mm to 15mm length, support 2kg load distributed across three compartments, cost under $30 to produce, and feature minimalist aesthetic. Final evaluation honestly reported: successfully accommodated specified size range (achievement), load test revealed minor deformation under 2kg suggesting future versions require internal reinforcement (shortcoming), production cost reached $28 within budget (achievement), and minimalist aesthetic matched intentions (achievement). This balanced evaluation demonstrated realistic assessment.

### Example
> "Against stated design criteria: The prototype successfully accommodates items from 15mm to 180mm length, exceeding the 150mm minimum specification (achievement). Load testing revealed the 2kg distributed load produces 1.2mm deformation—acceptable for non-structural applications but suggesting future versions would benefit from internal diagonal bracing if load-bearing performance requires improvement. Production cost achieved $27 per unit, within the $30 budget constraint (achievement). Aesthetic evaluation against minimalist goals proved successful, with user feedback confirming visual appeal aligns with contemporary design preferences. Future refinement priorities would focus on load-bearing capacity enhancement if weight distribution changes."

## Client/User Feedback

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: user testing, evaluation, design refinement, design validation

### Definition
Client or user feedback involves soliciting responses from target users or project stakeholders regarding prototype performance, usability, aesthetic appeal, and overall satisfaction. Feedback collection methods include informal conversations (initial impressions), structured interviews (exploring specific experiences), questionnaires (quantifying satisfaction metrics), and extended usage testing (observing how prototypes function in real contexts). Feedback provides validation that designs actually serve intended users.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment values evidence that you've sought actual user input rather than assuming designs are successful. User feedback often reveals surprises—aspects you thought important might prove irrelevant, whilst unforeseen problems emerge in actual use. Demonstrating responsiveness to feedback by incorporating improvements shows design thinking. Additionally, including user feedback in your folio proves the design wasn't merely self-indulgent but actually serves stated target users.

### Scenario
A student designing a kitchen organiser system completed the prototype believing she'd successfully solved identified user problems. Testing with actual target users revealed unexpected discoveries: organisation scheme worked differently than designed (users grouped items by frequency of use, not by item category as the design assumed), colour choice considered "sophisticated" by the designer was found "sterile" by users who preferred warmer tones, and the system's complexity (which the designer valued for comprehensiveness) overwhelmed users preferring simpler approaches. This feedback drove productive refinement.

### Example
> "I conducted user testing with 8 members of my target market (busy professionals aged 30-50), observing them using the prototype for 30 minutes each and completing questionnaires rating usability (1-10 scale), aesthetic appeal, and likelihood to purchase. Feedback revealed: 7/8 users successfully completed intended tasks (positive validation), 6/8 preferred slightly warmer colour palette than my original choice, all users appreciated comprehensive compartmentalisation (positive for design approach), and 5/8 requested minor adjustments to compartment arrangement. This feedback confirmed core design approach whilst identifying specific refinements enhancing user satisfaction."

## Presentation of Major Design Project

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: design folio, communication and design, presentation skills, professional standards

### Definition
Presentation of the Major Design Project encompasses both the design folio (written documentation) and the physical prototype (tangible evidence of design work). Presentation quality includes folio layout and visual communication, prototype condition and finishing quality, and presentation narrative—the story communicated through combined folio and prototype. Professional presentation respects audience expectations for polished, well-organised work.

### Why It Matters
Your design folio and prototype represent the culmination of months of work; presentation quality directly influences how assessors perceive the underlying design. A brilliant design presented poorly in a dishevelled folio or unfinished prototype loses impact compared to good design presented excellently. The Major Design Project assessment includes marks for presentation quality—justifiably, because design professionals must communicate effectively. Investing time in polishing folio presentation and prototype finishing maximises the impact of your design work.

### Scenario
A student completed excellent design and fabrication work but submitted a hastily assembled folio with inconsistent page layouts, blurry photographs and cramped text. A peer with equivalent design work invested final weeks in professional folio production—consistent layouts, high-quality photography, clear typography and strategic whitespace. Despite identical design quality, the professionally presented folio received significantly higher marks because presentation effectively communicated design excellence.

### Example
> "I completed design folio presentation using professional page layout software: consistent two-column grid across all 80 pages, unified typography hierarchy (Helvetica Neue 18pt headings, 11pt body text), limited colour palette supporting readability, strategic whitespace preventing overcrowding, and 45 professional-quality photographs clearly supporting narrative. Physical prototype received final finishing: careful sanding progression to 320-grit smoothness, application of three oil finish coats buffed between applications, and protective storage preventing damage to finished surfaces. This attention to presentation ensured both folio and prototype communicated design excellence clearly."
