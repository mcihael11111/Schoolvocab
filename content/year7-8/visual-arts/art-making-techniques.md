## Hatching

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Cross-hatching, Tone, Line, Drawing techniques

### Definition
Hatching is a drawing technique where parallel lines are placed close together to create tone and shadow. The closer together the lines, the darker the value; spacing them creates lighter tones.

### Why It Matters
Hatching is one of the most fundamental drawing techniques and appears in classical art history from Renaissance to contemporary work. Mastering hatching allows you to create tone and form using only line, making you a more versatile and confident drawer.

### Scenario
When learning to shade a sphere, Jesse discovered that hatching felt more controlled than trying to blend charcoal. By carefully placing parallel lines in the shadow areas, varying the spacing as the sphere curved, Jesse achieved a convincing three-dimensional effect. The technique felt like drawing had given him a superpower—he could create realistic form with just a pen.

### Example
> "Old master drawings like those by Rembrandt used sophisticated hatching to create dramatic lighting and deep, expressive shadows."

## Cross-Hatching

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Hatching, Tone, Texture, Drawing techniques

### Definition
Cross-hatching is a drawing technique where overlapping layers of parallel lines are drawn in different directions to create tone, shadow, and texture. Each layer adds darkness and visual interest.

### Why It Matters
Cross-hatching is a more flexible technique than simple hatching and allows for greater tonal range and visual texture. Understanding cross-hatching gives you a powerful tool for creating dramatic, sophisticated drawings across any subject matter.

### Scenario
During an observational drawing of an orange, Sam began with simple hatching but found it looked flat. When she added lines in different directions, layering the marks, the orange suddenly looked dimensional and textured. The cross-hatching created the appearance of the bumpy orange skin while also showing light and shadow.

### Example
> "Contemporary artists like Kate Beaton still use cross-hatching in their illustrations and comics to create tone and texture with remarkable efficiency."

## Stippling

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Pointillism, Tone, Patience, Mark-making

### Definition
Stippling is a drawing and painting technique where tiny dots are placed next to each other to create tone and form. The density and spacing of dots determine the value—more dots create darker areas, fewer dots create lighter areas.

### Why It Matters
Stippling teaches patience, precision, and control. It transforms a laborious process into a meditative practice that produces remarkably detailed, expressive work. This technique demonstrates that texture and tone don't require blending or shading tools.

### Scenario
When Mira began a stippled portrait using a single pen, she expected it to be tedious. Instead, she found herself in a state of focus where the repetitive dots became almost meditative. As hours passed and the image gradually emerged from thousands of tiny marks, she felt connected to her artwork in a way drawing quickly never achieved.

### Example
> "George Seurat developed pointillism, a fine art version of stippling, where individual dots of colour optically blend to create luminous paintings."

## Blending

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Tone, Form, Soft edges, Charcoal techniques

### Definition
Blending is a shading technique where adjacent tones are smoothly merged together using blending tools (stumps, tissues, fingers, or brushes) to create soft transitions and subtle gradations of tone.

### Why It Matters
Blending creates soft, realistic effects that convey smooth surfaces and gentle lighting. Understanding when and how to blend—and when not to blend—is essential for creating work that ranges from highly realistic to expressively loose.

### Scenario
When learning charcoal drawing, Hassan experimented with different blending approaches. For a portrait, he blended facial tones smoothly to show skin texture. For a dramatic landscape, he left harsh lines unblended to suggest storm clouds. He discovered that blending wasn't always the answer—the quality of edges (soft or hard) communicated mood and meaning.

### Example
> "Sfumato, the blending technique perfected by Leonardo da Vinci, creates such soft transitions that edges seem to dissolve, producing luminous, atmospheric paintings."

## Wet-on-Wet Painting

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Watercolour, Fluid technique, Spontaneity, Transparency

### Definition
Wet-on-wet painting is a watercolour technique where paint is applied to wet paper or wet paint, allowing colours to spread, merge, and blend naturally without hard edges. The artist surrenders some control to the fluid movement of pigment.

### Why It Matters
Wet-on-wet painting teaches spontaneity and working with materials' natural properties rather than against them. This technique produces luminous effects impossible with other methods and connects you to centuries of watercolour tradition.

### Scenario
During an experimental watercolour workshop, Ollie sprayed his paper with water and dropped colour onto the wet surface. The pigment bloomed and flowed in surprising directions. Rather than fighting the medium's nature, Ollie learned to respond to what was happening—guided the colours, encouraged certain effects, and accepted the unpredictability as part of the artform.

### Example
> "J.M.W. Turner's luminous, atmospheric watercolours rely on wet-on-wet technique to create the hazy, dreamlike quality of light and water."

## Dry Brush Painting

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Texture, Expressive mark-making, Control, Oil painting

### Definition
Dry brush painting is a technique where paint with minimal water or medium is applied with a stiff, often scrubbed, brush to create rough, textured marks that show the weave of the canvas or paper underneath.

### Why It Matters
Dry brush creates expressive texture and energy in painting. This technique allows precise control while maintaining visible brushwork and raw, authentic mark-making. It's essential for creating dynamic, expressively painted artwork.

### Scenario
When Tanya switched from smooth, blended painting to dry brush, her work suddenly felt alive. Using stiff brushes and thickly loaded paint, she dragged colour across the canvas, leaving streaks and texture that showed the canvas beneath. The apparent roughness paradoxically made her paintings feel more true and authentic.

### Example
> "Andrew Wyeth uses meticulously controlled dry brush technique to create paintings that feel both photorealistic and intensely personal through visible, deliberate brushwork."

## Wash Technique

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Watercolour, Glazing, Transparency, Tone

### Definition
The wash technique involves applying broad areas of diluted colour (paint thinned with water or medium) to create large zones of tone and colour. Washes can be applied in layers, allowing previously applied washes to show through.

### Why It Matters
Wash technique is fundamental to watercolour painting and helps you understand transparency and layering. It teaches efficient use of materials and creates the characteristic luminous quality of transparent media.

### Scenario
For a landscape study, Kenji applied a warm yellow wash across the entire paper for the sky. Once dry, he applied a blue wash for distant mountains, allowing the yellow to show through, creating a luminous green in the overlap. Each wash built the painting without covering previous layers entirely—a transparent, efficient approach that watercolour does better than any other medium.

### Example
> "Winslow Homer's watercolour seascapes use wash technique to capture light reflecting on water with remarkable economy and transparency."

## Layering and Glazing

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Transparency, Depth, Oil painting, Watercolour

### Definition
Layering and glazing involves building an artwork from thin, transparent layers where each new layer modifies the colours and tones underneath. Each layer typically dries before the next is applied.

### Why It Matters
Layering creates depth and luminosity impossible with single-layer work. Understanding how colours interact when layered—optically mixing—allows sophisticated colour effects. This fundamental technique appears across all painting media.

### Scenario
During oil painting lessons, Zara began with an underpainting in monochrome, establishing form and value. She then built transparent colour glazes—thin layers of coloured oil paint—over this foundation. As she layered transparent red over blue, the colours seemed to glow rather than appear flat. The layering created internal light within the painting.

### Example
> "Old Masters like Vermeer built up paintings through dozens of transparent glazes, creating luminous effects that appear to come from within the painting itself."

## Printmaking: Relief Printing

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Carving, Positive and negative space, Edition printing, Linocut

### Definition
Relief printing is a process where an image is carved or cut into a surface (wood, linoleum, or other material), removing material around the image. The remaining raised surface is inked and pressed onto paper to create a print.

### Why It Matters
Relief printing connects you to centuries of printmaking tradition and teaches important principles about positive and negative space. The process of carving forces deliberate, confident mark-making and produces bold, graphic results.

### Scenario
When Leon carved a linocut for the first time, he felt nervous about carving away material he might need. His teacher explained that relief printing requires commitment—once you carve it, it's gone. This taught Leon to think carefully before acting, to plan his composition, and to trust his artistic decisions. The resulting bold, graphic print had an energy that came from that committed mark-making.

### Example
> "Expressionist artists like Emil Nolde created raw, emotionally powerful woodcuts using bold carving strokes that express inner feeling through the physical act of cutting."

## Monoprinting

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Printing, Spontaneity, One-off artwork, Expressive

### Definition
Monoprinting is a printmaking technique where paint is applied to a smooth surface (glass or metal plate) and drawn or manipulated, then paper is pressed onto the inked surface to create a one-of-a-kind print. Because each print is unique, it's called a "mono" print—one print.

### Why It Matters
Monoprinting combines the spontaneity of painting with the physicality of printing. It teaches you to work backwards (creating mirrored images) and produces fresh, energetic artwork that bridges printmaking and painting.

### Scenario
During a monoprinting session, Ava rolled black ink across a glass plate and used a stick to draw through the ink, revealing the glass underneath. She could see the image in reverse on the glass. When she pressed paper onto the inked plate, lifted it away, and turned it over, the image appeared correctly. The surprise of seeing the final print emerge felt magical.

### Example
> "Contemporary printmakers use monoprinting to create loose, expressive artwork that shows immediate response to materials without the control of planned editions."

## Sculpture: Modelling

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Form, Three-dimensional form, Clay, Additive sculpture

### Definition
Modelling is a sculptural technique where soft material (usually clay) is built up and shaped by hand, gradually forming a three-dimensional object. Material can be added, removed, and reshaped throughout the process.

### Why It Matters
Modelling develops three-dimensional thinking and teaches you to work in the round—considering all angles and sides. Working with malleable material develops intuitive understanding of form and allows direct response to materials.

### Scenario
When working with clay for the first time, Imran was surprised at how responsive the material was to touch. He could add coils, smooth surfaces, carve lines, and adjust proportions continuously. Unlike drawing on flat paper, sculpture required constant rotation and viewing from all angles. His hands understood form in a way his eyes alone never could.

### Example
> "Henry Moore's bronze sculptures began as clay models, developing the organic, abstract forms for which he's renowned, always considering the void and space within form."

## Sculpture: Construction and Assemblage

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Three-dimensional form, Found objects, Welding, Conceptual art

### Definition
Construction and assemblage are sculptural techniques where an artwork is built by joining separate pieces or found objects together. Materials might be welded, glued, bolted, or otherwise assembled to create a three-dimensional structure.

### Why It Matters
Construction teaches problem-solving, planning, and structural thinking. Assemblage gives artistic significance to ordinary objects, teaching you that art doesn't require precious materials—intention and concept matter more than material.

### Scenario
For a sustainability project, Nina collected discarded materials—old bicycle parts, wire, plastic bottles—and welded them into an abstract sculpture. The construction process required planning how pieces would hold together and how forces would balance. The final sculpture carried environmental meaning: beauty and art could emerge from our discarded waste.

### Example
> "David Smith created welded steel sculptures that revolutionised sculpture as an artform, proving that industrial materials could create expressive, beautiful work."

## Mixed Media

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Collage, Found objects, Experimentation, Contemporary practice

### Definition
Mixed media is an artwork created using a combination of different materials and techniques. An artwork might combine paint, collage, found objects, drawing, and three-dimensional elements in a single piece.

### Why It Matters
Mixed media reflects contemporary art practice where boundaries between traditional art forms have dissolved. Understanding mixed media expands your creative possibilities and encourages experimentation and innovation.

### Scenario
When given freedom to choose materials for a personal project, Sophia felt overwhelmed at first. Rather than limiting herself to one medium, she combined watercolour, charcoal, collage elements, ink, and glitter. Each element contributed unique qualities—the luminosity of watercolour, the precision of charcoal, the texture of collage, the spontaneity of ink drips. The mixed media approach expressed her ideas more fully than any single medium could.

### Example
> "Kurt Schwitters created assemblages combining painting, collage, and found objects, pioneering mixed media as a serious artform that valued accident and discovery."
