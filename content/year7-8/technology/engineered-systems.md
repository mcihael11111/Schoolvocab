## Simple Machines: The Lever

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Mechanical advantage, force, simple machines, inclined plane, pulley, effort, load

### Definition
A lever is a simple machine consisting of a rigid bar (the lever) that pivots around a fixed point (the fulcrum). By positioning the fulcrum at different distances from the load, you can multiply your force. A long lever with the fulcrum near the load requires less effort force to lift a heavy load.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding levers helps you appreciate mechanical advantage in everyday objects. A seesaw, crowbar, bottle opener, and wheelbarrow are all levers. Knowing why a longer crowbar is more effective than a short one shows understanding of how simple machines amplify human effort.

### Scenario
When Anna struggled to remove a tight lid from a paint can, she tried holding the edge directly. When that failed, she placed a wooden spoon under the lid edge with the can's rim as the fulcrum. The lever action made it much easier. Her understanding of lever mechanics solved the problem.

### Example
> "A 1-meter crowbar with the fulcrum 0.1 meters from the load has a mechanical advantage of 10:1. A 100-newton effort force (one hand push) lifts a 1000-newton load. A shorter 0.3-meter crowbar with the same ratio would require more effort for the same load."

## Simple Machines: Pulleys and Pulley Systems

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Mechanical advantage, force distribution, simple machines, fixed pulleys, movable pulleys, compound systems

### Definition
A pulley is a wheel with a grooved rim that a rope or belt passes through. Fixed pulleys (attached to a fixed point) don't provide mechanical advantage but change the direction of force—useful for lifting loads more easily upward. Movable pulleys attached to the load itself provide mechanical advantage: a movable pulley system multiplies force by distributing the load.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding pulley systems shows how mechanical advantage can be achieved. Flagpoles use pulleys, construction cranes use complex pulley systems, and even simple garden equipment uses pulleys. Recognising these applications shows practical awareness of mechanical engineering.

### Scenario
When a construction team needed to lift heavy building materials to upper levels, they used a pulley system instead of lifting directly. A four-pulley system meant four rope segments shared the load, so the required effort force was one-quarter of the load weight. Understanding pulleys made heavy lifting manageable.

### Example
> "A single fixed pulley changes direction: pulling down on one rope end lifts the load, with 1:1 force ratio. A movable pulley provides 2:1 mechanical advantage: pulling the rope with 100N lifts a 200N load. A compound system (four pulleys) provides 4:1 advantage."

## Simple Machines: Inclined Plane

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Mechanical advantage, force, friction, ramp, wedge, simple machines, gradient

### Definition
An inclined plane is a flat surface tilted at an angle. It's a simple machine that reduces the effort force needed to move a load to a higher elevation. Rather than lifting a heavy object straight up, moving it up a gentle slope requires less force (but more distance). The mechanical advantage depends on the angle: steeper planes require more force.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, inclined planes appear everywhere: ramps, stairs, slides, and conveyor belts. Understanding why a gentle ramp is easier than stairs (mechanical advantage vs steeper angle) shows practical mechanical knowledge. This principle explains everyday engineering design choices.

### Scenario
When helping his grandmother move boxes into an upstairs room, Leo considered two methods: carrying each box straight up the stairs (difficult), or building a temporary ramp at a gentle angle (easier). Understanding the inclined plane principle, he knew the ramp method would require less force despite taking longer.

### Example
> "Moving a 1000N box straight up 3 meters requires 1000N of effort. Moving it up a 10-meter-long ramp (3-meter rise) requires only 300N of effort. The ramp provides 3.3:1 mechanical advantage. But the distance increases from 3 to 10 meters—trading distance for effort reduction."

## Simple Machines: Wheel and Axle

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Mechanical advantage, rotation, torque, simple machines, gears, pulley, wheel size

### Definition
A wheel and axle consists of a wheel (large diameter) mounted on an axle (smaller diameter cylinder). Applying force to the wheel (large radius) creates greater torque than applying the same force to the axle (small radius). This provides mechanical advantage. Larger wheels relative to axles provide greater advantage.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, wheel and axle systems are fundamental to machinery. Bicycle cranksets, door handles, screwdrivers, and car steering wheels all use this principle. Understanding why larger wheels are easier to turn helps explain everyday mechanical design.

### Scenario
When manually turning a machine with a crank handle, Chen compared crank sizes. A longer crank (larger wheel) required less force than a short crank (smaller wheel) to achieve the same rotation. His understanding of wheel and axle mechanics explained why handle design matters.

### Example
> "A steering wheel with 0.4m radius (large wheel) with a 0.02m steering column (small axle) creates a 20:1 mechanical advantage. Applying 50N force to the wheel rim creates 1000N torque at the axle, making the car easy to steer despite the heavy front wheels."

## Simple Machines: Screw and Wedge

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Mechanical advantage, inclined plane, rotation, fasteners, splitting, wedges, thread pitch

### Definition
A screw is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. The mechanical advantage comes from the pitch (distance the screw advances per rotation). A fine-pitched screw (many rotations per centimetre) provides high mechanical advantage—great effort amplification but slower penetration. A wedge is a triangular inclined plane used for splitting or adjusting position.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, screws are fundamental fasteners in almost every project. Understanding screw pitch helps you select appropriate fasteners: fine-pitched wood screws for precise positioning, coarse screws for quick assembly. Wedges appear in many applications from door stops to splitting wood.

### Scenario
When choosing fasteners for a delicate assembly, Sophia selected fine-pitched screws (high mechanical advantage) allowing precise positioning. For speed-critical assembly later, she switched to coarse-pitched screws (lower mechanical advantage but faster). Her understanding of pitch matched fasteners to requirements.

### Example
> "A wood screw with 2mm pitch advances 2mm per rotation. A 20mm long screw needs 10 rotations. A fine-pitch 1mm pitch screw needs 20 rotations for the same depth but provides more holding force due to greater mechanical advantage."

## Mechanical Advantage and Force Multiplication

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Simple machines, levers, pulleys, inclined planes, effort, load, distance, trade-offs

### Definition
Mechanical advantage is the ratio of output force (the load being moved) to input force (the effort applied). Simple machines provide mechanical advantage by multiplying force, though usually at the cost of increased distance. A 3:1 mechanical advantage means 1 newton of effort moves a 3-newton load.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding mechanical advantage helps you choose and design efficient systems. A high mechanical advantage (like 10:1) makes lifting heavy loads easy but requires multiple rotations or large movements. Low mechanical advantage (like 1:1) requires more effort but is faster. Design requires balancing these trade-offs.

### Scenario
When designing a lifting mechanism for a university competition, Yuki calculated mechanical advantages of different configurations. A 5:1 system meant lighter motors but slower lifting. A 2:1 system was faster but required more powerful motors. She selected based on competition requirements and available budget.

### Example
> "A basic hand hoist with 3:1 mechanical advantage: to lift a 300kg load, pull with 100kg of force. But pulling 30 meters of rope lifts the load only 10 meters. A 10:1 hoist requires only 30kg effort but needs 100 meters of rope for the same 10-meter lift."

## Gears and Gear Ratios

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Mechanical advantage, torque, speed, rotation, efficiency, power transmission, gear types

### Definition
Gears are toothed wheels that mesh together to transfer rotational force between shafts. Gear ratio is the relationship between the number of teeth: a small gear (10 teeth) meshing with a large gear (40 teeth) produces a 4:1 ratio. Different ratios provide different mechanical advantages and speed changes.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, gears appear in many systems: car gearboxes, bicycle derailleurs, clock mechanisms, and power tools. Understanding gear ratios explains why a small gear driving a large gear multiplies torque (useful for heavy loads) whilst reducing speed—a fundamental concept in mechanical engineering.

### Scenario
When building a motorised winch for a design competition, Marcus selected gears to provide the right mechanical advantage. A 10:1 gear ratio allowed a small electric motor to lift heavy loads despite the motor not being powerful enough for direct drive. His gear selection optimised performance.

### Example
> "A 20-tooth gear meshing with a 40-tooth gear creates a 2:1 ratio. If the small gear rotates 1000 RPM, the large gear rotates 500 RPM. The large gear has 2× the torque but half the speed. Car transmissions use different ratios: low gear (high torque, low speed) for acceleration, high gear (low torque, high speed) for highway cruising."

## Electrical Circuits: Series and Parallel

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Current, voltage, resistance, circuit design, switches, load distribution, fault tolerance

### Definition
Electrical circuits can be configured in two ways: series (components connected in a single loop, current flows through each sequentially) and parallel (components connected on separate branches, current splits between paths). Series circuits are simpler but a single failure breaks the circuit. Parallel circuits continue functioning if one branch fails.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding circuit configuration is essential for designing reliable systems. Most homes use parallel circuits because losing one light bulb shouldn't turn off all lights. Series circuits are used when you need components to function together. Knowing when to use each shows electrical design thinking.

### Scenario
When designing a sensor array for a solar car, Nina configured most sensors in parallel so that one sensor failure wouldn't disable the entire system. Critical sensors were in series so all had to function. Her design showed understanding of circuit architecture and reliability.

### Example
> "A series circuit with one 10-ohm and one 5-ohm resistor totals 15 ohms. Remove either resistor and the circuit breaks. A parallel circuit with the same resistors has two separate paths; current can flow through either. Loss of one path reduces current but maintains function."

## Electrical Circuits: Introduction to Ohm's Law

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Voltage, current, resistance, electrical safety, power, circuit design, troubleshooting

### Definition
Ohm's Law states that Current (I) = Voltage (V) ÷ Resistance (R), or V = I × R. This relationship shows that voltage pushes electrical current through resistance. Higher voltage increases current, higher resistance decreases current. Understanding this relationship helps design circuits with appropriate current and avoid dangerous situations.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, Ohm's Law is fundamental to electrical design. It explains why a short circuit (very low resistance) is dangerous (very high current), why resistance is needed to limit current through components, and how to calculate required resistances for different applications.

### Scenario
When designing an LED indicator circuit, Ethan used Ohm's Law to calculate resistance needed to limit current to safe levels. A 12V supply and 2V LED required: R = (12V - 2V) ÷ 0.02A = 500 ohms. His calculation prevented the LED from burning out due to excessive current.

### Example
> "A 12-volt circuit with 60-ohm total resistance draws 0.2 amps of current. If resistance increases to 120 ohms, current drops to 0.1 amps. If a short circuit (1-ohm resistance) occurs, current rises to 12 amps—potentially causing fire. That's why circuit breakers protect circuits."

## Sensors and Transducers

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Input devices, signal processing, control systems, electronic components, calibration, real-world data, IoT

### Definition
Sensors are devices that detect physical phenomena (temperature, light, motion, humidity) and convert them to electrical signals. Different sensors detect different things: thermistors detect temperature, photoresistors detect light, ultrasonic sensors detect distance, motion sensors detect movement. Sensors provide the input data for control systems.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, sensors are essential for automated systems and robotics. Understanding different sensor types and their applications helps you design intelligent systems that respond to real-world conditions. This knowledge connects electrical engineering with practical problem-solving.

### Scenario
When programming an automated greenhouse, Alejandra used multiple sensors: temperature sensors monitored heat, humidity sensors monitored moisture, light sensors monitored sunlight. Her controller used sensor data to automate watering and ventilation, creating an intelligent system.

### Example
> "A temperature sensor (thermistor) changes resistance with temperature; a light sensor (photoresistor) changes resistance with light level; an ultrasonic sensor measures distance by sending sound pulses; a motion sensor (PIR) detects movement. Each provides data for automated responses."

## Actuators and Output Devices

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Motors, solenoids, control systems, mechanical output, electrical to mechanical conversion, feedback loops

### Definition
Actuators are devices that convert electrical signals into mechanical movement or action. Motors convert electrical energy into rotational motion. Solenoids convert electrical energy into linear motion. LED lights provide light output. Buzzers provide sound output. Actuators are how control systems affect the physical world.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding actuators is essential for robotics and automation projects. Knowing that a motor provides continuous rotation whilst a solenoid provides controlled linear movement helps you select appropriate actuators for your application. This knowledge bridges electronics and mechanical engineering.

### Scenario
When designing a robot arm for a competition, Jordan selected actuators carefully: servo motors for precise angular positioning of arm joints, a linear solenoid for the gripper (open/close), and a motor for base rotation. Each actuator suited its specific function.

### Example
> "A DC motor connected to a battery rotates continuously; speed is controlled by voltage. A servo motor positions at specific angles with electrical pulse control. A solenoid pushes a plunger forward when powered, springs return it when powered off. An LED provides light output. Each actuator serves different mechanical purposes."

## Pneumatic and Hydraulic Systems

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Fluid pressure, force transmission, power systems, actuators, efficiency, safety, compressibility

### Definition
Pneumatic systems use compressed air to produce movement and control mechanical systems. Hydraulic systems use pressurised fluid (typically oil). Both can transmit power effectively over distances. Pneumatic systems are simpler and cleaner but less powerful; hydraulic systems are more powerful but require more maintenance.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding fluid power systems helps you appreciate industrial and heavy machinery. Construction equipment, dental drills, air tools, and automatic doors use pneumatic systems. Heavy machinery like excavators and presses use hydraulic systems. Knowing these technologies exist shows awareness of diverse engineering solutions.

### Scenario
When designing an automated assembly line, Sophie chose pneumatic actuators for light assembly tasks (fast, clean, simple) and hydraulic actuators for heavy pressing operations (powerful enough for the loads). Her selection showed understanding of each system's strengths.

### Example
> "An air compressor creates pressurised air; pneumatic cylinders use this pressure to extend and retract. Excavators use hydraulic pumps to pressurise oil; hydraulic cylinders produce tremendous force for digging. Pneumatic systems are faster but weaker; hydraulic systems are slower but more powerful."

## Control Systems and Feedback Loops

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Sensors, actuators, automation, feedback, system response, stability, closed-loop systems, error correction

### Definition
A control system monitors conditions (using sensors), compares actual conditions to desired conditions, and adjusts actuators to maintain the desired state. A thermostat is a simple control system: it measures temperature, compares to the setpoint, and activates heating/cooling to maintain the target. Feedback loops allow continuous correction.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding control systems is fundamental to automation. From cruise control in cars to temperature control in ovens to modern smart homes, control systems are everywhere. Understanding closed-loop systems (with feedback) versus open-loop (without feedback) shows sophisticated systems thinking.

### Scenario
When programming an autonomous vehicle, Ray implemented a control loop: sensors measured distance to obstacles, a controller compared distances to safe thresholds, and actuators adjusted steering and speed. The continuous feedback loop kept the vehicle operating safely.

### Example
> "A simple thermostat: temperature sensor → compare to 20°C setpoint → if cooler, turn on heater; if warmer, turn off heater. The heater runs until temperature reaches 20°C, then shuts off. As room cools, it turns back on. This feedback loop maintains constant temperature automatically."

## Robotics and Automation Basics

- type: apply-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Sensors, actuators, control systems, programming, mechanical systems, problem-solving, design challenges

### Definition
Robotics combines mechanical systems, electronics, sensors, and programming to create automated devices. A robot typically has a structure (frame), actuators (motors), sensors (input), control electronics, and a programme. Robots can perform repetitive tasks, operate in hazardous environments, or assist humans with complex work.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, robotics projects demonstrate integration of multiple concepts: mechanical design, electrical systems, programming, and problem-solving. Robotics competitions and projects are valuable learning experiences that show how different technologies work together to solve real problems.

### Scenario
In the school robotics competition, Zara's team designed a robot to collect and sort objects. It integrated: a mechanical arm (actuator), colour sensors (input), an ultrasonic distance sensor (input), servo motors (controlled actuators), and a microcontroller (programme logic). Each component worked together.

### Example
> "A competition robot might include: wheels with motors (locomotion), an arm with servos (manipulation), light sensors (navigation), colour sensors (object detection), and a microcontroller running the programme. The system integrates mechanics, electronics, and code to perform autonomous tasks."
