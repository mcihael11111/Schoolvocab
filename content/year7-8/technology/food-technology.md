## Nutrients and Food Groups

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Nutrition, balanced diet, food pyramid, macronutrients, micronutrients, health, dietary requirements

### Definition
Nutrients are substances in food that your body needs for growth, energy, and health. Macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, fats) provide energy and building blocks. Micronutrients (vitamins, minerals) support body functions. The five food groups (vegetables, fruits, grains, proteins, dairy) provide balanced nutrition. A balanced diet includes appropriate portions from each group.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, understanding nutrition is fundamental to designing healthy recipes and meals. Knowing that different food groups provide different nutrients helps you create balanced dishes. This knowledge connects food preparation to health outcomes—important for both personal wellbeing and professional food service.

### Scenario
When planning meals for a sports team, Carmen considered nutritional requirements: adequate protein for muscle recovery, carbohydrates for energy, healthy fats for hormonal function, and vegetables for vitamins and fibre. Her understanding of nutrients ensured meals supported athletic performance.

### Example
> "A balanced breakfast might include: wholegrain toast (carbohydrates, fibre), scrambled eggs (protein, fat), berries (vitamins, minerals), and milk (calcium, protein). Each food group contributes specific nutrients, creating nutritional balance."

## Food Safety and Hygiene Practices

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Bacteria, food poisoning, contamination, hygiene standards, handwashing, storage, sanitation, HACCP

### Definition
Food safety involves preventing bacterial contamination that causes food poisoning. Key practices include washing hands before food preparation, keeping raw and ready-to-eat foods separate, maintaining clean surfaces and utensils, and proper food storage. Good hygiene prevents foodborne illness and ensures food safety.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, food safety is non-negotiable. Food poisoning can be serious, especially for vulnerable groups. Demonstrating understanding of hygiene practices shows responsibility and professionalism. In any food-related career, food safety knowledge is essential and expected.

### Scenario
When preparing meals at a school fundraiser, Sarah implemented strict safety practices: washed hands before and after handling raw meat, used separate cutting boards for raw and ready-to-eat foods, and kept potentially hazardous foods at appropriate temperatures. Her practices prevented any food safety issues.

### Example
> "Proper food handling: wash hands for 20 seconds with soap before food prep, use separate cutting boards for raw meat (prevents cross-contamination), keep raw chicken below 4°C, and don't let prepared food sit at room temperature longer than 2 hours."

## Cooking Methods: Dry Heat Techniques

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Heat transfer, food transformation, browning reactions, texture changes, technique selection, equipment

### Definition
Dry heat cooking uses air or direct heat without liquid: baking (dry heat in an oven), grilling (direct radiant heat), roasting (dry heat cooking in an oven), and frying (cooking in hot fat). Dry heat methods often create browning reactions that develop flavour and create crispy textures. Temperature and timing significantly affect results.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, mastering dry heat techniques is essential for product development and technical cooking skills. Understanding how dry heat creates browning reactions (Maillard reaction) and develops flavours shows sophisticated cooking knowledge. Different techniques suit different products.

### Scenario
When developing a gluten-free cookie recipe, Daniel experimented with dry heat techniques. Baking at different temperatures and times affected browning, texture, and flavour. He discovered that lower temperatures (160°C) produced better results than higher temperatures (180°C) for his recipe, understanding how dry heat affected his specific product.

### Example
> "Baking a cake: dry heat in an oven (180°C) causes flour proteins to set, eggs to coagulate, and sugar to caramelize, creating a cake structure. Roasting vegetables: dry heat creates browning (flavour development) and concentrates sweetness. Grilling meat: radiant heat creates a crust and develops flavour."

## Cooking Methods: Moist Heat Techniques

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Water, steam, heat transfer, tenderness, nutrient retention, cooking times, braising, boiling, steaming

### Definition
Moist heat cooking uses water or steam: boiling (food immersed in hot water), steaming (food above boiling water in steam), poaching (food simmered gently in liquid), and braising (slow cooking in a small amount of liquid). Moist heat is gentler than dry heat and often produces tender results, though some nutrients may leach into the cooking liquid.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, moist heat techniques are essential for products where tenderness is important. Knowing that boiling root vegetables until soft takes 15-20 minutes, whilst steaming takes 10-15 minutes, helps you plan recipes effectively. Understanding how cooking liquids can carry away flavour and nutrients informs cooking choices.

### Scenario
When creating a vegetable side dish for a cooking competition, Maya compared moist heat methods: boiling, steaming, and braising. She discovered steaming retained more colour and nutrients than boiling, and braising in stock created more developed flavour. Her technique choice balanced nutrition and taste.

### Example
> "Boiling broccoli in water until tender (10 minutes) cooks it thoroughly but loses water-soluble vitamins in the cooking water. Steaming for 8 minutes retains more nutrients. Braising in stock develops flavour whilst keeping vegetables moist. Each method suits different dishes."

## Cooking Methods: Combination Techniques

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Dry heat, moist heat, technique integration, browning, tenderness, flavour development, complex dishes

### Definition
Combination cooking uses both dry and moist heat: braising starts by searing meat in a hot pan (dry heat) to develop flavour, then finishes by simmering in liquid (moist heat). Pot roasting combines roasting and braising. These techniques combine the browning and flavour development of dry heat with the tenderising of moist heat.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, combination techniques produce sophisticated results: tough cuts of meat become tender through long moist cooking whilst developing rich flavour through initial searing. Understanding when and how to combine techniques shows advanced cooking knowledge and enables creation of complex, flavourful dishes.

### Scenario
When developing a beef stew recipe, Chris wanted tender meat with rich flavour. He combined techniques: seared beef chunks in a hot pan first (dry heat browning), then braised them in stock with vegetables (moist heat tenderising). The combination produced meat that was both tender and flavourful.

### Example
> "A pot roast: sear a joint in a hot pan (create flavour crust), then add braising liquid and vegetables and cook slowly in the oven (tenderise while developing complex flavour). The finished dish has browning and flavour from searing, tenderness and sauce from braising."

## Food Preservation Methods

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Bacterial growth, shelf life, storage, refrigeration, freezing, canning, fermentation, food safety, sustainability

### Definition
Food preservation prevents spoilage by slowing or stopping bacterial growth. Methods include: refrigeration (slows bacterial growth), freezing (stops bacterial growth), canning (eliminates bacteria through heat and sealing), drying (removes water bacteria need), fermentation (uses beneficial bacteria), and salting (draws out water). Different methods suit different foods.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, understanding preservation extends the value of fresh foods and reduces waste. Knowing why blanching before freezing vegetables prevents enzyme activity, or why fermented foods develop beneficial probiotics, shows sophisticated food science knowledge. This knowledge is valuable for food product development.

### Scenario
When developing jams for a school market stall, Elena researched preservation. She used traditional canning (heat sterilisation plus sealing) to preserve fruit jams safely for several months without refrigeration. Her understanding of preservation allowed shelf-stable products.

### Example
> "Freezing vegetables stops bacterial growth, preserving quality for months. Canning uses heat to eliminate bacteria and seals the jar to prevent recontamination. Drying removes water that bacteria need to grow. Fermentation uses beneficial bacteria to preserve food whilst adding probiotics. Each method suits different products."

## Ingredient Functions and Interactions

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Chemistry, recipe development, ingredient selection, functional substitution, baking science, emulsification, gelling

### Definition
Different ingredients perform specific functions in recipes: flour provides structure (gluten), eggs bind and aerate, sugar sweetens and affects texture, butter provides richness and contributes to browning, raising agents (baking powder, yeast) create lift. Understanding these functions helps you understand why recipes work and make informed substitutions.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, understanding ingredient functions enables flexible recipe development and substitution. Knowing that baking soda requires acid to activate, that salt enhances flavour, or that eggs emulsify fat and liquid shows scientific understanding of cooking. This knowledge enables problem-solving when ingredients are unavailable.

### Scenario

Her knowledge of ingredient functions solved a real problem.

### Example

## Raising Agents and Fermentation

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Ingredient functions, yeast, bacteria, chemical reactions, gas production, dough development, baking science

### Definition
Raising agents create the lift in baked goods: chemical raising agents (baking soda, baking powder) create carbon dioxide through chemical reactions, whilst biological raising agents (yeast, sourdough cultures) produce gas through fermentation of sugars. Each requires different conditions and timing to function effectively.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, understanding raising agents is crucial for successful baking. Knowing that baking soda requires immediate baking (the reaction starts immediately), whilst yeast requires time for fermentation, helps you select appropriate agents and manage timing in recipes. This knowledge prevents common baking failures.

### Scenario
When baking scones, Rachel used baking powder (a chemical raising agent) requiring immediate mixing and baking. For bread, she used yeast (biological raising agent) requiring hours of fermentation. Her understanding of different raising agents suited each application.

### Example
> "Baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) plus acid (lemon juice, buttermilk) produces immediate carbon dioxide. Baking powder contains baking soda plus acid; adding water activates it. Yeast consumes sugar over hours, producing carbon dioxide slowly for bread rise. Each produces lift through different mechanisms."

## Emulsification and Thickening Agents

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Chemistry, ingredient functions, sauces, emulsions, mayonnaise, béchamel, thickening, texture

### Definition
Emulsifiers combine two ingredients that don't normally mix: fat and water. Eggs, mustard, and lecithin are emulsifiers used in sauces like mayonnaise. Thickening agents (starch, gelatin, pectin) trap water and increase viscosity, creating sauces, gravies, and gels. Understanding these functions helps you control texture.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, understanding emulsification and thickening enables control over sauce texture and consistency. Knowing why mayonnaise can separate if whisked too vigorously, or why stirring flour into hot liquid prevents lumps, shows practical food science knowledge valuable in recipe development.

### Scenario
When creating a vegan hollandaise sauce without traditional emulsifiers (egg yolks), Danielle researched alternatives: lecithin could emulsify fat and water. Using lecithin, she created a stable sauce with the correct texture. Her understanding of emulsification science solved the challenge.

### Example
> "Mayonnaise emulsifies oil and vinegar/egg yolk: the lecithin in eggs allows oil droplets to suspend in water. Béchamel thickens with flour roux (flour plus fat): starch granules gelatinise in hot liquid, creating thickness. Gelatin thickens by trapping water as it cools and sets."

## Temperature Control and the Danger Zone

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Food safety, bacteria growth, refrigeration, heating, food poisoning, time-temperature control, cooking temperatures

### Definition
The temperature danger zone (4-60°C) is where bacteria grow rapidly. Cold temperatures (below 4°C) slow bacterial growth (cold storage). High temperatures (above 60°C) kill bacteria (hot holding). Foods in the danger zone for extended periods become unsafe. Critical control points in food service involve rapid temperature changes—chilling quickly, heating thoroughly.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, understanding temperature danger zone is essential food safety knowledge. Knowing why you shouldn't leave cooked food at room temperature, why raw chicken must be kept below 4°C, and why heating to 75°C kills bacteria shows safety awareness. This knowledge prevents foodborne illness.

### Scenario
When catering a school event, Isabella ensured food safety: kept raw chicken below 4°C (slowing bacterial growth), heated it to 75°C during cooking (killing bacteria), and served hot food above 60°C or kept it chilled below 4°C (preventing bacterial growth). Her temperature management prevented food poisoning.

### Example
> "Bacteria multiply rapidly in the danger zone (4-60°C). Raw chicken at 20°C grows dangerous bacteria quickly; at 2°C it stays safe for weeks. Cooking to 75°C kills pathogens. Serving above 60°C or chilling below 4°C prevents growth. Never leave cooked food at room temperature longer than 2 hours."

## Food Sustainability and Sourcing

- type: think-about-it
- level: beginner
- related: Environmental impact, local sourcing, seasonal produce, food miles, ethical production, waste reduction, sustainability

### Definition
Food sustainability involves producing food with minimal environmental impact: reducing food miles (sourcing locally), using seasonal produce (lower transportation), supporting ethical farming practices, and reducing food waste. Sustainable sourcing benefits the environment whilst often supporting local producers and communities.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Food Technology, demonstrating sustainability awareness shows contemporary thinking. Modern consumers increasingly value sustainable food choices. Understanding that locally-sourced seasonal produce has lower environmental impact than air-freighted produce out of season shows awareness of food systems and environmental responsibility.

### Scenario
When designing a school canteen menu, Victoria prioritised local, seasonal produce: supporting local farmers with lower food miles, offering seasonal fruits and vegetables (lower environmental impact and better prices), and reducing food waste through careful menu planning. Her approach benefited the environment and the budget.

### Example
> "A sustainable food choice: buying apples from a local farm 20km away versus imported apples from overseas (reducing food miles and emissions). Seasonal strawberries in summer versus heated greenhouse strawberries in winter (reducing energy use). These choices support sustainability."
