## Design Brief

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Design challenge, design criteria, constraints, stakeholder analysis

### Definition
A design brief is a written document that outlines the design task, including the problem to be solved, the target user, and any constraints or requirements. It serves as the foundation for all design decisions and helps keep the project focused.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, understanding how to interpret a design brief is crucial because it directly guides all your design work. A clear brief ensures you're solving the right problem and helps you evaluate whether your final product actually meets the client's needs.

### Scenario
Sarah received a design brief from her school canteen to create a new lunch container that keeps hot food warm for three hours while being easy to carry in a backpack. She read through the brief carefully, noting the temperature requirement, portability constraint, and target user (high school students). This understanding shaped every design decision she made.

### Example
> "Design a water bottle that secondary school students will use during sports, that costs less than $20 to manufacture, holds 750ml, and must be durable enough to survive being dropped from 1.5 metres."

## Ideation Techniques

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Brainstorming, creative thinking, design brief, sketching, divergent thinking

### Definition
Ideation is the process of generating multiple creative ideas and solutions to a design problem. Techniques like mind mapping, lateral thinking, and random word association help you explore many possibilities before committing to one design direction.

### Why It Matters
Ideation is essential in Design & Technology because it teaches you to think beyond obvious solutions and develop innovative ideas. Many good designs come from exploring a range of ideas first, and this skill is directly assessed in HSC projects.

### Scenario
When designing a storage solution for his bedroom, Marcus used a mind-mapping technique where he started with "storage" in the centre and branched out into ideas like vertical storage, hanging solutions, drawer systems, and multifunctional furniture. This helped him discover a creative solution combining a bed frame with built-in drawers.

### Example
> "Using the 'six thinking hats' method, a design team explored a phone holder for a car. Red hat: emotional response (frustration when phones fall); white hat: facts (phones are 100-200g); green hat: creative ideas (magnetic mount, suction cup, air vent clip)."

## Brainstorming

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: Ideation techniques, creative thinking, group work, design process, constraints

### Definition
Brainstorming is a collaborative technique where a group generates ideas without immediate criticism or evaluation. The goal is quantity over quality, encouraging wild ideas and building on others' suggestions to create innovative solutions.

### Why It Matters
Brainstorming teaches you to think creatively and collaboratively, skills essential for HSC Design & Technology projects. It demonstrates that good design solutions often emerge from combining different perspectives and building on multiple ideas rather than working individually.

### Scenario
A Year 8 class brainstormed ideas for a "keeping food fresh" project. During the session, students called out ideas without judgment: plastic containers, paper bags, beeswax wraps, glass jars, bamboo boxes, and even a solar-powered cooling device. Later, they evaluated which ideas were practical and which sparked new directions.

### Example
> "In a brainstorming session for school uniform improvement, team members suggested: different fabric options, adjustable sizing, pockets in unexpected places, reversible designs, and even colour-changing technology—some unrealistic, but some led to the final practical design."

## Sketching for Design Communication

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Drawing techniques, design communication, 2D representation, 3D visualisation, annotated sketches

### Definition
Sketching is a quick, visual way to explore and communicate design ideas. Design sketches are usually rough, annotated drawings that show dimensions, materials, colour, and construction details—not artistic masterpieces, but functional visual communication.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, sketching is a critical communication tool for expressing your design thinking. Examiners and clients can quickly understand your ideas through sketches, and the act of sketching often helps you solve design problems you hadn't spotted in written descriptions.

### Scenario
When designing a pencil holder for a desk, Lia sketched several quick variations: one cylindrical, one with compartments, one with a hexagonal shape. She annotated each with dimensions, materials (timber, plastic, metal), and construction methods. These sketches helped her team decide which direction was most feasible.

### Example
> "A design sketch for a backpack might include measurements (45cm height × 30cm width), annotations for fabric type (water-resistant nylon), zip placement, pocket positions, and a note that straps should be adjustable from 40-60cm."

## Prototyping

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Iterative design, testing, evaluation, materials selection, feedback, refined prototype

### Definition
A prototype is a working model of your design that allows you to test how it actually functions in real life. Prototypes don't need to be perfect or final—they're tools for learning what works and what needs improvement.

### Why It Matters
Prototyping is central to HSC Design & Technology assessment. A prototype demonstrates that you can move from theory to practical making, and shows evidence of problem-solving and refinement. Examiners expect to see prototyping as part of your design journey.

### Scenario
For his jewellery stand design, Jamal made a first prototype from cardboard and dowels in just two hours. Testing it revealed that earrings would fall off the small hooks. He refined it, creating a second prototype with larger, angled hooks. This iterative approach resulted in a much more functional final product.

### Example
> "A prototype for a phone stand might be 3D printed in rough plastic first, allowing the designer to test the angle, stability, and the size of the gripping area before making the final version in timber with a polished finish."

## Testing and Evaluation

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Prototype, performance criteria, user feedback, refinement, design brief, quantitative/qualitative testing

### Definition
Testing involves checking whether your prototype meets the design criteria and functions as intended. Evaluation is the process of analysing the test results and deciding what changes need to be made in the next iteration.

### Why It Matters
In HSC projects, testing and evaluation evidence is often what separates excellent submissions from adequate ones. This process shows rigorous design thinking and demonstrates that you can critically assess your own work and make evidence-based improvements.

### Scenario
Emma tested her homemade lip balm formulation on five volunteers, tracking whether it remained on the lips for two hours, how it felt, and any skin reactions. She recorded results in a table and discovered that increasing beeswax by 5% solved the problem of it melting too quickly in warm weather.

### Example
> "For a tablet stand, testing might involve: holding it at the specified angle for 10 minutes (ergonomic comfort), dropping it from 1 metre (durability), checking if a 500g weight stays secure (stability), and asking five users if they'd actually buy it (user satisfaction)."

## Iterative Design Process

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Prototyping, testing, evaluation, refinement, design cycle, feedback loops

### Definition
Iteration is the process of repeating the design-test-evaluate-improve cycle multiple times. Each iteration builds on what you learned from the previous one, gradually moving closer to an ideal solution. Rarely is the first design the best.

### Why It Matters
Understanding iteration is fundamental to HSC Design & Technology because it shows maturity in your design thinking. Assessment rubrics explicitly look for evidence that you've refined your design multiple times based on feedback and testing, not just made one version.

### Scenario
When creating a study lamp, Raj made four different versions: first prototype (light too dim), second (adjusted bulb angle), third (better heat management with ventilation holes), and final (with added features based on user feedback about portability). Each iteration was an improvement.

### Example
> "An app designer might iterate ten times: version 1 has unclear navigation buttons; version 2 adds labels; version 3 reorganises the menu; version 4 changes colours based on user testing; and so on until users say it's intuitive."

## Design Specifications

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Design brief, performance criteria, constraints, manufacturing requirements, material specifications

### Definition
Design specifications are detailed technical requirements that describe exactly what the finished product must do, how big it should be, what materials should be used, and how it should perform. They're written in measurable terms so success can be clearly evaluated.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, specifying your design clearly is crucial for evaluation. Writing specifications helps you understand exactly what you're trying to achieve and gives you clear targets to test against. Vague designs are difficult to assess and often fail to meet requirements.

### Scenario
For a wooden puzzle game, Maya's design specifications included: dimensions (20cm × 20cm × 5cm), mass (less than 500g), material (pine timber), finish (satin varnish), edge radius (minimum 3mm), sixteen puzzle pieces, target age (6-10 years), and assembly time (less than 2 minutes).

### Example
> "A water bottle specification might read: 'Capacity: 750ml ±20ml, mass: 150-200g, material: food-grade plastic, lid must seal at 2.5 atmospheres, survive 0.5m drop test, fit in a standard cup holder, and be suitable for hot liquids up to 80°C.'"

## Constraints in Design

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Design brief, specifications, problem-solving, trade-offs, budget, materials, time

### Definition
Constraints are the limitations or restrictions you must work within when designing. Common constraints include budget (cost limit), materials available, time to complete the project, space restrictions, or environmental requirements.

### Why It Matters
Understanding constraints is critical in HSC Technology because real-world design always has limitations. Learning to work creatively within constraints teaches you problem-solving and resourcefulness—both essential design skills. Constraints actually often lead to better, more innovative solutions.

### Scenario
When designing a school lunch container, Aisha faced these constraints: budget of $8 per unit, must use recyclable materials, can't exceed 30cm in any dimension, must hold hot food without becoming too hot to touch, and must be assembled in under 3 minutes. These constraints pushed her toward an insulated two-chamber design.

### Example
> "A teenage entrepreneur designing a bookshelf has constraints: budget $30, uses only reclaimed wood, must support 20kg per shelf, fits in a 150cm × 100cm space, and must be built in one weekend. Each constraint requires creative problem-solving."

## User-Centred Design

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Target users, empathy, user research, accessibility, inclusive design, user feedback

### Definition
User-centred design means designing with the actual user's needs, abilities, and preferences at the forefront. Instead of designing what you think is good, you research who will use the product and make decisions based on their real needs and constraints.

### Why It Matters
In HSC projects, demonstrating user-centred design shows sophisticated design thinking. It moves beyond technical competence to show that you understand design is fundamentally about solving real people's problems. This perspective often leads to more innovative and commercially viable solutions.

### Scenario
When designing a lunch container, Priya interviewed twelve secondary school students about their actual lunch habits: where they ate, how long lunch breaks were, what foods they brought, temperature preferences, and ease-of-use requirements. Her design directly addressed these real needs rather than her assumptions.

### Example
> "A university library upgraded their study space using user-centred design by observing students for two weeks, interviewing 30 about their needs, noting they wanted whiteboards, better lighting, and quiet zones separate from collaborative areas. The renovation reflected these observed needs perfectly."

## Empathy Mapping

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: User-centred design, user research, user personas, stakeholder analysis, user needs, design brief

### Definition
Empathy mapping is a visual technique where you put yourself in the user's shoes and create a map showing what they see, hear, think, feel, say, and do. It helps designers deeply understand user motivations and constraints beyond what people explicitly tell you.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, creating empathy maps demonstrates sophisticated user research. It shows you're thinking about the emotional and practical dimensions of design, not just functional requirements. This level of insight often results in more commercially successful and personally meaningful designs.

### Scenario
When designing a study timer for students, Jackson created an empathy map for a typical Year 10 student. He noted: sees (school workload, social media notifications), hears (friends playing games), thinks (will I pass this test?), feels (anxious about exams), says (I'll study later), does (procrastinates). This understanding shaped his timer's motivational features.

### Example
> "An empathy map for a wheelchair user designing a library might reveal: sees (shelves at standard height), hears (silence expected), thinks (others are watching), feels (frustrated), says nothing (doesn't want to burden staff), does (leaves without books). Good design addresses all these dimensions."

## Feedback Loops in Design

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Iterative design, testing, user feedback, evaluation, continuous improvement, design communication

### Definition
A feedback loop is a system where information about how your design is performing gets collected, analysed, and used to make improvements. Feedback can come from users, testing, expert review, or self-evaluation—and the loop continues through multiple cycles.

### Why It Matters
In HSC projects, understanding feedback loops shows that you recognise design as an ongoing process, not a one-time event. Many students make one prototype and move on; demonstrating multiple feedback cycles shows maturity and sophistication that's recognised in assessment rubrics.

### Scenario
Leah designed a recipe website and showed it to five target users (teenagers aged 13-17). They gave feedback: ingredients list was confusing, search function didn't work as expected, and mobile layout was hard to navigate. She improved the site based on this feedback and tested with five different users, completing a full feedback loop.

### Example
> "A chocolate company using feedback loops: makes a batch with a new flavour, gives samples to 100 customers, collects feedback via survey, analyses results, adjusts recipe, makes another batch, tests again. This cycle repeats until customer satisfaction is very high."

## Design Criteria and Performance Standards

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Design specifications, design brief, testing, evaluation, measurable requirements, success factors

### Definition
Design criteria are the specific standards your design must meet to be successful. Unlike vague goals, criteria should be measurable (e.g., "must keep food hot for 3 hours" not just "should keep food warm"). Performance standards detail the acceptable level of quality for each criterion.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Assessment, having clear design criteria is essential because it gives you targets to design toward and provides clear evidence of success. Assessors look for criteria that are specific, measurable, and actually related to the design brief—vague criteria suggest unclear thinking.

### Scenario
For a school shoe design, Karim established these criteria: must be comfortable for six-hour wear (comfort rating 8+/10 from wearers), must support the foot during running (120kg load test), must last two years (accelerated wear testing), must cost under $40 to manufacture, and must appeal to Year 7-12 students (survey result 7+/10).

### Example
> "A backpack design criteria: capacity 15-20L, mass when empty under 500g, fabric water-resistance (minimum 500mm column test), stitching durability (1000 cycle seam test), strap adjustment range 40-60cm, and ability to carry 10kg of books safely."

## Problem Analysis and Definition

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: Design brief, user needs, constraints, research, design challenge, stakeholder analysis

### Definition
Problem analysis is investigating and understanding a design challenge before jumping to solutions. It involves researching who will use the product, what the actual problem is (not just your assumption), what's already been tried, and what constraints exist.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Technology, strong problem analysis is the foundation of strong design. Students who rush to design without understanding the problem often create solutions to the wrong question. Taking time to properly analyse the problem shows rigorous thinking and usually results in better final designs.

### Scenario
When asked to design a storage solution for art supplies, Lily didn't immediately sketch containers. Instead, she interviewed art teachers and students, observed how they currently stored supplies, researched existing solutions, and identified that the real problem wasn't storage capacity but quick accessibility during lessons. Her analysis shaped a fundamentally different solution.

### Example
> "Analysing school lunch waste, a designer discovered the problem wasn't that containers were bad, but that food was prepared in bulk and some sat too long. This analysis led to a solution involving pre-portioning and time labels, not a container redesign."

## Design Communication and Documentation

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Sketching, annotations, specifications, portfolio, design process recording, technical drawings

### Definition
Design communication is presenting your design thinking, decisions, and solutions to others through sketches, written descriptions, annotations, prototypes, and technical drawings. Good communication makes your thinking visible to others and is essential for feedback and assessment.

### Why It Matters
In HSC projects, how well you communicate your design journey is as important as the product itself. Examiners can't see your thinking unless you document it. Strong documentation—with sketches, notes, test results, and reflection—demonstrates the depth of your design thinking and separates excellent projects from basic ones.

### Scenario
For her Technology submission, Nina created a design portfolio including: annotated sketches showing iteration, photographs of three prototypes, test data on a spreadsheet, user feedback notes, and written reflections on what she'd learned. This comprehensive documentation clearly showed her design journey.

### Example
> "A strong design communication might include: initial concept sketches with measurements, isometric drawing of the final design, material specifications list, photograph of the prototype with labels showing construction techniques, and a table comparing original design criteria to testing results."
