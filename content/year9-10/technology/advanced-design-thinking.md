## Human-Centred Design Philosophy

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: User research, empathy, user needs, inclusive design, accessibility, design ethics, stakeholder analysis

### Definition
Human-centred design places people at the core of design decisions. Rather than solving technical problems first and adapting them to users, human-centred design starts with deep understanding of users' actual needs, constraints, and context. This approach often leads to more usable and valuable solutions that users genuinely want.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, human-centred design is increasingly the industry standard. Projects explicitly assessed on whether they address genuine user needs receive higher marks than technically competent solutions to assumed problems. This shift reflects real-world design practice where understanding users is crucial.

### Scenario
Instead of assuming elderly people wanted a simplified computer interface, Raj conducted research: observed how seniors actually interacted with technology, interviewed them about barriers, and watched them struggle with existing solutions. His research revealed the real problem wasn't simplicity but accessibility—large text, high contrast, voice control. His design addressed actual needs.

### Example
> "An app designer spent time watching how teenagers actually used public transport (users often distracted, rushed, standing with limited attention), rather than assuming they just needed a timetable. This insight led to a design with large, scannable information, voice notifications, and one-tap journey planning."

## Systems Thinking and Complex Problem-Solving

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Systems design, interconnections, emergent properties, unintended consequences, holistic thinking, stakeholder impact

### Definition
Systems thinking recognises that complex problems have multiple interconnected causes and effects. Solving one problem might create others unless the entire system is understood. Systems thinkers map how components interact, identify feedback loops, and anticipate unintended consequences of design changes.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, systems thinking enables sophisticated problem-solving. Simple product-focused design often fails because it ignores system context. A designer who understands how their product fits in the broader system—affecting other products, user behaviours, and environmental outcomes—creates more effective and responsible solutions.

### Scenario
When designing a plastic-free lunch container, Amelia considered the entire system: raw material sourcing (renewable resources?), manufacturing process (clean?), user behaviour (will they actually use it?), disposal (is recycling infrastructure available?), and economic viability (can it be profitably manufactured?). Her systems view produced a viable solution.

### Example
> "A plastic bag ban seems simple: reduce plastic use. But systems thinking reveals complexities: consumers use paper bags (not reusable), produce loses freshness (food waste increases), alternative bags require production (environmental cost), and some users can't afford reusable bags (equity concern). Effective solutions address the entire system."

## Design for Manufacturability and Scalability

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: Manufacturing processes, cost reduction, quality consistency, production scaling, prototype to production, supply chain, feasibility

### Definition
Design for manufacturability (DFM) involves designing products that can be manufactured efficiently at scale. This means considering: tool costs, material waste, assembly complexity, quality consistency, and production speed. Designs that work as one-off prototypes might be impossible to manufacture profitably at scale or might have quality issues in production.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, demonstrating understanding of manufacturability shows professional design thinking. Many student projects look good as single prototypes but would be impossible to manufacture commercially. Projects explicitly addressing how designs would scale to production, and optimising for manufacturing constraints, show mature thinking.

### Scenario
When Kai designed a timber product, he initially created an intricate hand-crafted prototype. Analysing manufacturability at scale, he redesigned for CNC machining: simplified geometry, fewer unique parts, standard material sizes, and repeatable processes. His refined design could be profitably manufactured whilst maintaining quality.

### Example
> "A phone design with hand-assembled components might cost $500 to produce in small quantities. Designing for automated assembly, injection-moulded plastic parts, and standardised components reduces production cost to $20 per unit at scale. DFM transforms ideas into viable products."

## Iterative Prototyping and Rapid Development

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: Prototyping methods, testing cycles, feedback integration, MVP (minimum viable product), agile design, refinement speed

### Definition
Iterative prototyping creates multiple versions in quick succession, each incorporating feedback from the previous. Rather than spending months perfecting one prototype, iterative approaches make rough prototypes quickly, test them, get feedback, and improve. Rapid prototyping enables fast learning and refinement.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, demonstrating rapid iteration shows modern design thinking. Industry uses iterative approaches because they reduce time to market and improve final products. Projects showing evidence of multiple quick iterations, user feedback incorporated into each, demonstrate sophisticated design practice.

### Scenario
When developing an app, Sophia created a rough prototype in one week, showed it to users and received feedback, refined it in another week, tested again, and completed five full iteration cycles in a month. Her rapid approach identified and fixed issues that months of isolated development wouldn't have caught.

### Example
> "Prototype 1 (week 1): basic functionality, tested with users, feedback shows navigation confusing. Prototype 2 (week 2): navigation redesigned, tested, feedback shows layout hard on mobile. Prototype 3 (week 3): responsive design added, tested, refinement continues. Rapid iteration finds problems early when they're easy to fix."

## Rapid Prototyping Technologies

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: 3D printing, laser cutting, CNC machining, digital fabrication, prototyping speed, design iteration, technology access

### Definition
Rapid prototyping technologies (3D printing, laser cutting, CNC machining) transform digital designs into physical prototypes in hours rather than days or weeks. This enables fast iteration: design, fabricate, test, refine, repeat. Different technologies suit different materials and design complexity—3D printing for complex shapes, laser cutting for flat materials, CNC for precision.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, access to rapid prototyping technologies significantly enhances project quality. Students with access to 3D printers or laser cutters can iterate rapidly, testing and refining designs multiple times. Projects demonstrating rapid prototyping show sophisticated design practice aligned with industry standards.

### Scenario
With a 3D printer available, Marcus iterated through seven prototypes of a mechanical component in two weeks. Each test revealed problems: first was too weak, second had interference issues, third needed wall thickness adjustment. By prototype seven, it was optimal. Without rapid prototyping, he might have stopped at prototype one.

### Example
> "Traditional prototyping: design wooden component (week 1), hand craft prototype (week 2), test it, identify problems (week 3), hand craft improved version (week 4). Rapid prototyping: design (day 1), 3D print (day 2), test and iterate (day 3-5), improved design (day 6), 3D print again (day 7). Same refinement in half the time."

## Design for Sustainability and Circular Thinking

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Lifecycle assessment, circular economy, sustainable materials, waste reduction, environmental impact, long-term thinking

### Definition
Designing for sustainability means considering the product's full lifecycle impact: material sourcing (renewable? recycled?), manufacturing (clean? efficient?), product lifespan (durable? repairable?), end of life (recyclable? biodegradable?), and broader impact (social responsibility? ecosystem protection?). Circular design aims to eliminate waste.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, explicitly designing for sustainability shows contemporary environmental awareness. Projects addressing sustainability through material selection, durability design, and end-of-life considerations receive recognition. This reflects that real-world design increasingly requires sustainability responsibility.

### Scenario
When designing a water bottle, Natasha specified: recycled ocean plastic (waste recovery), durable polycarbonate (decades of use), replaceable cap/seal (repairability extends life), and design for disassembly (enabling material recovery). Her comprehensive approach addressed sustainability at every lifecycle stage.

### Example
> "Sustainable shoe design: organic cotton and recycled polyester uppers (renewable/waste-based), plant-based glues (avoiding toxic chemicals), modularity enabling sole replacement (extending life by years), global supply chain certified for fair labour, and take-back programme for recycling at end-of-life."

## Biomimicry and Nature-Inspired Design

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Innovation, nature-inspired solutions, biological systems, sustainable design, problem-solving, design analogies

### Definition
Biomimicry uses patterns and strategies from nature to solve design problems. Velcro mimics burrs clinging to fur, bullet trains mimic kingfisher diving streamlines, aircraft wings use feather principles, and efficient building cooling mimics termite mounds. Studying nature often reveals elegant solutions to problems humans struggle with.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, biomimicry demonstrates sophisticated innovation thinking. Finding inspiration in nature for sustainable, efficient solutions shows that good design often already exists—designers need to observe and adapt it. This approach often leads to surprisingly elegant and sustainable solutions.

### Scenario
When designing a water collection system for arid regions, Chen studied how desert beetles collect water from fog: their bumpy shell surface catches fog droplets which roll to the mouth. Inspired, he designed bumpy material that catches water from humid air, requiring no moving parts or energy—elegant simplicity learned from nature.

### Example
> "Lotus leaves are naturally water-repellent (hydrophobic), inspiring stain-resistant fabrics. Abalone shells are incredibly strong through layered nacre structure, inspiring stronger ceramics. Mangrove root systems prevent erosion, inspiring coastal protection designs. Nature has solved many engineering challenges through evolution."

## Universal Design and Accessibility

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Inclusive design, accessibility, diverse users, disability awareness, design for all, user research, empathy

### Definition
Universal design creates products usable by the widest range of people, including those with disabilities. Rather than adding accessibility as an afterthought, universal design integrates it from the start. Curb cuts designed for wheelchair users benefit people with prams, elderly people with walkers, and people pushing shopping trolleys.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, designing inclusively shows ethical awareness and broader thinking. Projects explicitly designed for accessibility to diverse users—considering vision, hearing, mobility, cognitive considerations—demonstrate sophisticated design thinking. This approach also reaches larger markets, making it commercially smart.

### Scenario
When redesigning a public website, Daya considered accessibility from the start: text sizing adjustable (vision impairment), keyboard navigation (motor impairment), audio descriptions (vision impairment), clear language (cognitive accessibility), and high contrast options (vision impairment). Her universal design served everyone better.

### Example
> "Voice control (designed for accessibility) now benefits everyone using phones while driving. Captions (designed for deaf users) help people watching videos in noisy environments. Adjustable text size (designed for low vision) helps everyone in different lighting. Universal design benefits all users."

## Design Ethics and Responsible Innovation

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Ethical responsibility, social impact, user agency, power dynamics, unintended consequences, diverse perspectives, accountability

### Definition
Design ethics considers the broader impact of design decisions: Who benefits? Who might be harmed? What unintended consequences might occur? Does the design respect user autonomy? Are there equity or access issues? Ethical designers consider all stakeholder perspectives, including those least powerful in the system.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, explicitly considering design ethics shows maturity. Rather than just asking "will this work?", ethical designers ask "is this fair? responsible? respectful?". This perspective reflects that design is inherently a choice between possibilities, each with different impacts on different people.

### Scenario
When designing an algorithm to determine loan eligibility, Sarah recognised potential harms: historical bias in training data could disadvantage minorities, lack of transparency could make denials seem arbitrary, poor appeal processes could harm vulnerable people. Her ethical analysis shaped design decisions addressing these concerns.

### Example
> "A fitness tracking app designed without ethics might motivate unhealthy obsession with weight or exercise. An ethical design balances motivation with mental health, includes diverse body types, provides professional mental health resources, and avoids shaming. Ethics shapes design decisions."

## Innovation versus Invention

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Creativity, novelty, market value, adoption, diffusion, improvement, breakthrough, practical implementation

### Definition
Invention is creating something entirely new that didn't exist before. Innovation is improving existing things or finding new applications for existing ideas. Most successful products are innovations (improvement) rather than inventions (wholly new). Innovations are more likely to be adopted because they build on familiar foundations.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding this distinction prevents wasted effort on complex inventions when clever innovations would be more valuable. A slightly better solution that users actually adopt is more successful than a perfect solution nobody wants. This thinking encourages pragmatic, market-aware design.

### Scenario
When developing a product for her school fair, Mia considered: inventing an entirely new device (risky, nobody might want it) versus innovating an existing product type (lower risk, familiar to users). She innovated a lunch container, adding features users actually wanted rather than inventing something nobody needed.

### Example
> "The smartphone was an innovation combining existing technologies (touchscreen, processor, camera, wireless connection) into a new form, rather than a wholly new invention. Post-it notes were an innovation applying adhesive chemistry in a new way. Most valuable products are innovations, not inventions."

## Intellectual Property and Design Protection

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Patents, trademarks, copyrights, design rights, intellectual property law, innovation protection, commercialisation

### Definition
Intellectual property (IP) protects creative and innovative work: patents protect inventions (20-year exclusive use), copyrights protect creative works (books, art), trademarks protect brand names, and design registrations protect product appearance. IP protection enables creators to commercialise innovations without immediate copying.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding IP basics shows awareness of how innovations are protected commercially. Knowing that good ideas require IP protection to remain commercially viable, and that IP systems enable investment in innovation, connects design to business realities. Students producing innovative work should understand IP options.

### Scenario
When developing an innovative water treatment device, Marcus researched IP protection: a patent application (costing $2000-5000) would protect his invention, making it commercially valuable. Without patent protection, a competitor could copy his design immediately. His IP awareness informed commercialisation strategy.

### Example
> "A patent on a new manufacturing process prevents others using it for 20 years. A trademark on 'Nike' brand prevents confusion with other shoes. Copyright on design drawings protects against copying. Design registration protects the unique appearance of a product. Each IP type serves different protection purposes."

## Design Portfolio and Project Documentation

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Communication, design process recording, evidence collection, visual presentation, professional standards, narrative

### Definition
A design portfolio documents the complete design journey: research and analysis, ideation and concept development, prototyping and testing, iterations and refinement, final design, and reflection. Strong portfolios clearly show design thinking and decision-making, not just final products. Documentation uses sketches, photos, data, and written explanation.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, project portfolio quality significantly impacts assessment. Examiners can't see your thinking unless you document it. Comprehensive portfolios showing research, multiple iterations, testing data, and reasoned decisions receive much higher marks than submissions showing only final products.

### Scenario
When submitting her design project, Yuki included: initial research notes, empathy maps, ten concept sketches with analysis, prototype photos at three stages with test results, iteration justifications, CAD drawings, material specifications, and reflective writing explaining design choices. Her comprehensive documentation showed sophisticated design thinking.

### Example
> "Strong design portfolio: 20-page document with research section (user interviews, competitive analysis), ideation section (10 sketches with selection rationale), prototyping section (3 prototypes with test results and improvements), final design section (CAD, specifications, manufacturing plan), and reflection section."
