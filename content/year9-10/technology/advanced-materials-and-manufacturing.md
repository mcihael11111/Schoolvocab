## Smart Materials and Responsive Systems

- type: know-it
- level: advanced
- related: Material science, responsive systems, properties, shape-memory alloys, piezoelectric materials, thermochromic polymers

### Definition
Smart materials change properties in response to stimuli: shape-memory alloys (SMAs) return to original shape when heated (used in aircraft winglets, medical devices), piezoelectric materials generate electricity when deformed (used in sensors, energy harvesting), and thermochromic materials change colour with temperature (used in mood rings, temperature sensors). These materials enable innovative applications.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding smart materials opens possibilities for innovative design. Rather than passive products responding only to user input, smart materials enable products that adapt to conditions: clothing that changes insulation with temperature, buildings that adjust colour to manage heat, devices that sense and respond to environmental conditions.

### Scenario
When designing athletic wear, Emma researched smart fabrics: phase-change materials that absorb/release heat maintaining stable temperature, colour-changing fibres responding to body temperature, and moisture-wicking smart textiles. Her material selection enabled adaptive performance clothing without bulky insulation.

### Example
> "Shape-memory titanium returns to original shape when heated; used in aircraft winglets that change shape for efficiency. Piezoelectric ceramics generate voltage when bent; used in touch-sensitive surfaces. Thermochromic textiles change colour with temperature; used in temperature-sensitive clothing and medical applications."

## Nanomaterials and Nanotechnology

- type: know-it
- level: advanced
- related: Material science, properties, scale, applications, carbon nanotubes, nanoparticles, graphene, safety considerations

### Definition
Nanomaterials have dimensions smaller than 100 nanometres (billionths of a metre), giving them unique properties. Carbon nanotubes are extremely strong and conductive. Graphene is an incredibly strong single layer of carbon. Nanoparticles of metals, ceramics, and polymers have different colours and properties than bulk materials. Nanotech applications range from stronger materials to medical imaging.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding nanotechnology shows awareness of emerging material science. Nanomaterials enable new capabilities: incredibly strong yet lightweight composites, transparent conductive coatings, highly efficient catalysts, and targeted medical treatments. This knowledge shows awareness of technological frontier areas.

### Scenario
When designing lightweight sports equipment, David researched carbon nanotube composites: materials with carbon nanotubes embedded in plastic, providing strength and electrical conductivity at low weight. His material selection achieved performance that traditional materials couldn't match.

### Example
> "Carbon nanotubes (single-wall) have tensile strength 300 times stronger than steel, density one-sixth of steel. Graphene is single-layer carbon with remarkable electrical and mechanical properties. Nanoparticle gold appears red instead of gold due to size. Nanomaterials enable innovations impossible with bulk materials."

## Composite Materials: Carbon Fibre and Fiberglass

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Material engineering, strength-to-weight ratio, reinforcement, layup processes, applications, manufacturing

### Definition
Composite materials combine fibres (carbon, glass, aramid) with a matrix resin. Carbon fibre composites offer extremely high strength-to-weight ratio—critical for aircraft, sports equipment. Fiberglass offers good properties at lower cost. Layup determines strength (aligned fibres strengthen that direction, random fibres provide overall strength). Manufacturing processes include hand layup, vacuum infusion, and autoclave curing.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding composites shows awareness of advanced materials. Many modern products (aircraft, racing cars, sports equipment, prosthetics) use composites. Understanding that strength-to-weight ratio enables lightweight products, that layup affects directional strength, and that manufacturing affects properties shows materials sophistication.

### Scenario
When designing a prosthetic leg for a design challenge, Natasha selected carbon fibre composites: extremely strong yet lightweight (essential for prosthetics), directional fibre orientation for specific strength, and proven durability in this application. Her material selection addressed real user requirements.

### Example
> "Carbon fibre composites in a tennis racket: face weave (multi-directional strength for impact), shaft layers (aligned longitudinally for stiffness, circumferential for torsion resistance). Layup is engineered for specific performance. Aircraft use carbon composites for 80% weight reduction versus aluminium."

## CNC Machining and Precision Manufacturing

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: Computer control, precision, material removal, tool paths, CAM software, tolerance, efficiency, industrial production

### Definition
CNC (Computer Numerical Control) machines remove material using rotating tools: mills (cutting surfaces), lathes (turning), drills (creating holes). CAM software converts 3D designs into tool paths. Precision is exceptional—tolerance ±0.1mm possible. CNC enables rapid production of complex parts, essential for manufacturing.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, CNC machining enables bridge from prototype to production. Hand-made prototypes don't scale to manufacturing; CNC enables efficient, consistent production. Understanding CNC capabilities and limitations helps design for manufacturability. Projects including CNC-produced components show manufacturing awareness.

### Scenario
When scaling a prototype from hand-made to manufactured, Marcus redesigned for CNC: used standard tool sizes (reducing tool changes), simplified geometry (reducing cutting time), specified realistic tolerances (±0.5mm, not ±0.1mm), and optimised tool paths. His design changes reduced manufacturing cost 10-fold.

### Example
> "A CNC mill converts 3D CAD model to tool paths: tool moves to position, spindle rotates, tool plunges and feeds, creating desired shape. Complex shapes that would take hours of hand work complete in minutes. CNC enables low-cost high-volume production impossible with manual methods."

## 3D Printing and Additive Manufacturing

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Rapid prototyping, layer-by-layer construction, design freedom, material options, tolerance, post-processing, sustainability

### Definition
3D printing builds objects layer-by-layer from digital designs: FDM melts and extrudes plastic filament, SLA uses ultraviolet light to solidify resin, SLS sinters powder into shape, and powder-binder systems bind particles. Each technology has advantages—FDM is affordable, SLA produces fine details, SLS requires no support structure. Rapid iteration enables fast design refinement.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, 3D printing enables rapid prototyping and custom manufacturing. Unlike CNC (subtractive), additive manufacturing creates complex geometry impossible by subtraction. Small-batch custom products become viable. Understanding 3D printing capabilities and limitations shapes design choices.

### Scenario
When developing intricate jewellery designs, Sophie used 3D printing: created digital designs, printed multiple iterations in resin, refined based on feel and appearance, and produced master models for metal casting. 3D printing enabled design exploration impossible with traditional methods.

### Example
> "FDM printer builds plastic layer-by-layer (0.2mm per layer); affordable, good for simple shapes. SLA printer uses ultraviolet laser to solidify resin layer-by-layer; produces fine details. SLS printer sinters powder layer-by-layer without supports. Each technology suits different applications and tolerances."

## Laser Cutting and Precision Fabrication

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Cutting technology, precision, digital design, material compatibility, speed, versatility, design opportunity

### Definition
Laser cutting uses high-energy focused light to cut, etch, or engrave materials: wood, acrylic, cloth, leather, rubber. Vector-based designs direct the laser path. Cut edges are clean with minimal waste. Thickness and material type affect cut quality. Laser cutting enables rapid fabrication of flat parts from digital designs, essential for prototype development and small-batch production.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, laser cutting enables rapid fabrication of sophisticated designs. Vector art or CAD designs convert directly to physical objects. Complex patterns, intricate details, and tight tolerances are achievable quickly. Projects using laser cutting show design sophistication and modern fabrication methods.

### Scenario
When developing packaging for a product, Finn designed laser-cut boxes from corrugated cardboard: complex fold patterns to eliminate glue, custom geometry fitting the product perfectly, and quick fabrication enabling design iteration. Laser cutting transformed his cardboard designs into reality.

### Example
> "Laser cutter receives vector design: outline path tells laser where to cut, power and speed determine cut depth. A 3mm acrylic design cuts cleanly in one pass. A 6mm design may require multiple passes. Complex nested designs optimise material usage and reduce waste."

## Quality Assurance and Tolerance Management

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Precision, specification compliance, measurement, consistency, problem-solving, process control, statistical methods

### Definition
Quality assurance verifies manufactured parts meet specifications. Tolerances define acceptable variation: tight tolerances (±0.1mm) require precise manufacturing, loose tolerances (±1mm) are easier to achieve. Statistical process control monitors production consistency. Measuring equipment (calipers, coordinate measuring machines, gauges) verifies dimensions. SPC identifies and corrects process drift before defects.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding quality control shows responsibility. Manufacturing isn't perfect; all parts vary slightly. Specifying realistic tolerances, implementing measurement systems, and monitoring processes ensures consistent quality. Projects including quality data demonstrate manufacturing maturity.

### Scenario
When producing components on a CNC machine, Monica measured every tenth part, tracking dimensions in a control chart. When measurements drifted approaching tolerance limits, she adjusted tool offset before parts became defective. Her monitoring prevented scrap and maintained quality.

### Example
> "Part specification: 50mm ±0.5mm diameter hole. First run produces 49.9mm-50.1mm (good). After 100 parts, measurements drift to 50.4mm. Tool offset adjusted before reaching 50.5mm limit. Statistical monitoring prevents defects before they occur."

## Material Testing Methods and Analysis

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: Properties verification, strength testing, destructive testing, non-destructive testing, material science, standards

### Definition
Material testing verifies that materials meet specifications: tensile testing (pulling until failure) measures strength and ductility, hardness testing (indentation or impact) measures resistance to deformation, impact testing (sudden force) measures toughness, and durability testing (repeated cycles) measures fatigue resistance. Non-destructive testing (ultrasound, X-ray) checks for internal defects.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, material testing shows rigorous verification. Manufacturing requires confidence that materials perform as specified. Conducting tests (or understanding test results) demonstrates that design is evidence-based, not assumption-based. Projects including material testing data show scientific rigor.

### Scenario
Before approving a new material for a structural component, Elena conducted testing: tensile test (verify strength), impact test (verify toughness at working temperature), fatigue test (verify durability under repeated loading). Test results confirmed the material was appropriate.

### Example
> "Tensile test: pull material until breaking, measure stress and strain. Strong material breaks at high load (high tensile strength), ductile material stretches significantly before breaking, brittle material breaks suddenly. Hardness test: press diamond indenter, measure deformation size. Tougher material shows smaller indentation."

## Manufacturing Processes: Casting and Injection Moulding

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Production methods, part geometry, material selection, economics, design constraints, volume production

### Definition
Casting creates parts by pouring liquid material (metal, plastic) into moulds, cooling to solidify. Sand casting uses disposable sand moulds (low cost for few parts, rough surfaces). Investment casting uses ceramic moulds (high cost but fine detail). Injection moulding forces melted plastic into moulds at high pressure, ideal for large-volume production. Both enable complex geometry without subsequent machining.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding these production methods shapes design. Knowing that casting creates complex geometry easily but expensive moulds require high volumes, that injection moulding enables detailed fine geometry at scale, helps design decisions. Projects incorporating manufacturing process knowledge show practical awareness.

### Scenario
When deciding between manufacturing options, Raymond analysed: injection moulding required $50,000 tooling cost (worth only for 10,000+ units), sand casting required $5,000 and suited small volumes but gave rough surfaces, 3D printing required no tooling but was slow. His process selection matched production volume.

### Example
> "Casting: pour molten metal or plastic into mould, cool, remove part. Advantages: complex geometry, no subtractive waste. Disadvantages: casting defects possible, surfaces need finishing. Injection moulding: force plastic into mould under pressure, cool rapidly, eject. Advantages: fine detail, fast cycle. Disadvantages: high tooling cost, geometry limited by mould removal."

## Vacuum Forming and Thermoforming

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Plastic shaping, heating, forming process, tooling, mould design, surface finish, production volume

### Definition
Vacuum forming heats plastic sheet until soft, positions it over a mould, applies vacuum to suck plastic into mould shape, cools to solidify. Thermoforming is similar but uses pressure as well as vacuum. Both enable rapid production of plastic parts with complex shapes—used extensively in packaging, automotive, and consumer products. Minimal material waste compared to machining.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, thermoforming enables rapid iteration of plastic designs. Mould cost is much lower than injection moulding tooling, making small-batch production viable. Understanding the process shapes design: textured surfaces add strength, thickness variation affects strength and appearance, undercuts limit feasibility.

### Scenario
When prototyping a product housing, Georgia used vacuum forming: created a wooden mould, heated plastic sheet, formed it over the mould, and tested fit. Rapid iteration enabled design refinement. Once design was final, injection moulding tooling was justified for production.

### Example
> "Vacuum forming: heat plastic sheet to 120-160°C, position over mould, pull vacuum creating pressure difference, plastic forms against mould, cool to solidify. Cost: mould ~$1000 versus injection moulding $50,000. Volume: 100-1000 units suited to thermoforming; 10,000+ to injection moulding."

## Precision Measurement and Control Systems

- type: do-it
- level: advanced
- related: Tolerances, verification, accuracy, repeatability, measurement equipment, calibration, process control

### Definition
Precision manufacturing requires accurate measurement: micrometers measure to 0.01mm, dial gauges measure to 0.1mm, coordinate measuring machines (CMMs) verify complex geometry, and optical systems measure without contact. Measurements verify that processes produce conforming parts and identify when adjustment is needed. Calibration ensures measurement accuracy.

### Why It Matters
In HSC Design Innovation Technology, understanding measurement shows that manufacturing precision depends on verification. Without accurate measurement, tolerances are meaningless. Projects including measurement data and process verification demonstrate sophisticated manufacturing control and quality awareness.

### Scenario
When setting up CNC production, Imran measured first parts produced, identified small dimension drift (tool wear gradually changes dimensions), implemented offset adjustments in CAM programme, and remeasured to verify corrections. His measurement-driven approach maintained quality automatically.

### Example
> "Precision measurement: digital caliper (±0.1mm), micrometer (±0.01mm), dial gauge (±0.05mm), CMM (±0.05mm for complex geometry). Measurement identifies: process drift (gradual change requiring correction), tool wear (reduces dimension, requires tool offset adjustment), part damage (indicates process issues)."
