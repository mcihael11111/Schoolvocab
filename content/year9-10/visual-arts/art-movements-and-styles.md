## Impressionism

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Light and colour, Nature, Modern art, Art history

### Definition
Impressionism is an art movement originating in 1870s France, emphasising light, colour, and momentary perception. Impressionists painted outdoors, used visible brushstrokes, depicted ordinary subjects, and explored how light changed appearance across different times of day.

### Why It Matters
Impressionism revolutionised painting by prioritising perceptual experience over realistic representation. Understanding Impressionism helps you appreciate how 19th-century artists challenged academic traditions and laid foundations for modern art. The movement demonstrates how artistic innovation responds to new circumstances.

### Scenario
When studying Monet's Water Lilies series, Ethan recognised that the artist wasn't trying to paint reality photographically—instead, Monet captured subjective experience of light and colour. By painting the same subject at different times of day, Monet showed how perception changes. This approach transformed Ethan's understanding of what painting could do.

### Example
> "Monet's haystacks series demonstrates Impressionist principles: the same subject observed under different lighting conditions, emphasising light and colour over detail."

## Expressionism

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Emotion, Distortion, Inner experience, German art

### Definition
Expressionism is an early 20th-century movement emphasising emotional expression over realistic representation. Expressionists distorted forms, used bold, often non-naturalistic colours, and applied paint with visible energy to convey inner feeling.

### Why It Matters
Expressionism legitimised emotional communication in art. Understanding Expressionism helps you appreciate art's capacity to express inner states directly rather than describing external reality. Expressionist approaches remain influential in contemporary art.

### Scenario
When viewing Expressionist artworks, Kai was initially startled by the distorted forms and harsh colours. His teacher explained that distortion was intentional—these artists conveyed emotional intensity better through exaggeration than through realistic representation. Understanding this shifted Kai's aesthetic responses.

### Example
> "Egon Schiele's self-portraits convey psychological intensity through distorted forms, exaggerated features, and raw line work, expressing inner turmoil through visual distortion."

## Cubism

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Fragmentation, Multiple perspectives, Geometric abstraction, Modernism

### Definition
Cubism is an early 20th-century movement that fragmented subjects into geometric forms, showing multiple viewpoints simultaneously. Rather than depicting objects as they appeared from a single viewpoint, Cubists represented multiple perspectives on a single flat plane.

### Why It Matters
Cubism radically challenged Western representational traditions developed since the Renaissance. Understanding Cubism helps you recognise that visual representation is conventional, not natural. Cubist approaches influenced numerous subsequent movements and remain conceptually important.

### Scenario
When studying Picasso's Cubist portraits, Suki was confused at first—faces seemed broken apart and reconfigured. Her teacher explained that Cubism abandoned the single viewpoint rule, showing frontal, profile, and other angles simultaneously on a flat plane. This conceptual shift transformed how Suki understood representation.

### Example
> "Picasso's 'Les Demoiselles d'Avignon' fragmentizes the female form through multiple perspectives and geometric distortion, revolutionising how artists could approach representation."

## Surrealism

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Unconscious, Dreams, Symbolism, Absurdity

### Definition
Surrealism is a 20th-century movement exploring unconscious mind, dreams, and irrational imagery. Surrealists created surprising juxtapositions, painted dreamscapes, and employed techniques like automatism to bypass conscious control.

### Why It Matters
Surrealism explores art's psychological dimensions and the power of the irrational. Understanding Surrealism helps you appreciate that art can access emotional and psychological truths that rational thought cannot reach. Surrealist approaches inspire contemporary artists.

### Scenario
During a Surrealism study, Marcus painted his dreams as accurately as he could, creating impossible, irrational scenes. The process felt liberating—he could paint without worrying about representation's rules. Surrealism gave permission for irrationality and absurdity.

### Example
> "Salvador Dalí's melting clocks and surreal landscapes explore the unconscious, creating images that feel emotionally true even though they're visually impossible."

## Pop Art

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Mass culture, Consumerism, Commercial imagery, Contemporary culture

### Definition
Pop Art is a mid-20th-century movement celebrating mass culture, commercial imagery, advertising, celebrity, and consumer products. Pop artists used bright colours, commercial techniques, and repeated imagery from advertising and popular culture.

### Why It Matters
Pop Art elevated popular and commercial imagery to fine art status, challenging distinctions between high and low art. Understanding Pop Art helps you appreciate how contemporary art responds to mass culture. Pop approaches remain influential in contemporary practice.

### Scenario
When studying Warhol's Marilyn Monroe series, Jade recognised how Warhol transformed celebrity photographs into fine art through repetition, colour variation, and silkscreen printing. He wasn't critiquing celebrity culture—he was celebrating it while simultaneously making viewers think about originality, reproduction, and value.

### Example
> "Andy Warhol's repeated Campbell's Soup cans and Coca-Cola bottles elevate mass-produced consumer goods to fine art, questioning artistic authenticity and commercial value."

## Abstract Art

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Non-representation, Form and colour, Spirituality, Modernism

### Definition
Abstract art is non-representational artwork emphasising form, colour, line, shape, and composition rather than depicting recognisable objects. Abstract artists believed pure visual form could communicate meaning without referential content.

### Why It Matters
Abstract art represents a fundamental break from Western representational traditions. Understanding abstraction helps you appreciate that artworks can be entirely non-referential and still be meaningful. Abstract approaches open infinite artistic possibilities.

### Scenario
When first encountering abstract paintings, Oliver felt frustrated—he wanted to "know what it is." His teacher explained that abstract art invited formal response (reaction to colour, shape, composition) rather than narrative response. Learning to respond to pure form expanded his aesthetic appreciation.

### Example
> "Wassily Kandinsky created abstract paintings exploring how colours and forms communicate directly to viewers' emotions without representational reference."

## Realism

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Observation, Truth to life, Social commentary, Political art

### Definition
Realism is an art movement emphasising faithful representation of subjects as they appear, often focusing on ordinary people and scenes. Realist artists rejected idealisation and romantic interpretation in favour of accurate visual observation.

### Why It Matters
Realism demonstrates art's capacity for social commentary through honest representation. Understanding Realism helps you appreciate that accurate representation can be a powerful political stance. Realist approaches remain relevant in contemporary practice.

### Scenario
When studying Courbet's paintings of ordinary workers and peasants, Naomi recognised that choosing these subjects and painting them realistically was a political act. By elevating ordinary people to fine art subject matter, Realists challenged social hierarchies.

### Example
> "Gustave Courbet painted ordinary rural workers and peasants with the same formal attention Renaissance artists reserved for religious and aristocratic subjects, asserting democratic values."

## Street Art and Graffiti

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Public space, Illegality, Community, Contemporary practice

### Definition
Street art and graffiti refers to visual artworks created in public spaces, often illegally. Street art encompasses tags, murals, stencils, installation, and performance. Street artists address public audiences directly without institutional mediation.

### Why It Matters
Street art and graffiti democratise art, making it accessible to everyone regardless of institutional access. Understanding these practices helps you appreciate art outside traditional gallery systems. Street art often carries social and political messages.

### Scenario
When Akira began experimenting with street art, she recognised both its creative potential and ethical complexity. Creating art in public spaces without permission risked legal consequences but reached broader audiences than gallery spaces. She grappled with questions about whose spaces public spaces are.

### Example
> "Banksy's street art raises social and political questions through humour and visual wit, making gallery-system outsider art widely recognized and valued."

## Indigenous Australian Art Movements

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Cultural tradition, Dot painting, Symbolism, Contemporary Indigenous practice

### Definition
Indigenous Australian art movements include contemporary Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander artists' diverse practices emerging from traditional knowledge systems. Dot painting became internationally recognised, but contemporary Indigenous artists work across all media.

### Why It Matters
Understanding Indigenous Australian art movements is essential for Australian students. These movements demonstrate how traditional knowledge continues vibrant expression in contemporary forms. Respecting and learning from these traditions acknowledges Indigenous peoples' ongoing cultural significance.

### Scenario
When learning about contemporary Indigenous artists, Yuki was surprised that they used video, installation, digital media, and cross-media approaches alongside traditional forms. A visiting Indigenous artist explained that contemporary Indigenous culture evolves continuously—traditional knowledge expressed through contemporary forms remains authentically Indigenous.

### Example
> "Contemporary Indigenous artist Rover Thomas created powerful abstract paintings based on traditional Kimberley language and cultural knowledge, demonstrating how tradition informs contemporary practice."

## Contemporary Art

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Current practice, Conceptual art, Diversity, Global perspectives

### Definition
Contemporary art refers to artworks created currently, typically characterised by conceptual emphasis, media diversity, global perspectives, and questioning of established categories. Contemporary art often addresses social, political, and environmental concerns.

### Why It Matters
Understanding contemporary art prepares you for current artistic practice and ongoing innovation. Contemporary art's diverse, global nature helps you appreciate art beyond Western traditions. Engaging with contemporary art directly connects you to living artistic communities.

### Scenario
When researching contemporary artists from diverse global backgrounds, Hassan recognised that contemporary art is increasingly global and multicultural. Artists from different regions brought different perspectives, challenging Western-centric art history narratives.

### Example
> "Contemporary artists like Ai Weiwei, Kehinde Wiley, and Yayoi Kusama command international attention, representing contemporary art's global, diverse nature."

## Installation Art

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Space, Immersion, Viewer participation, Contemporary practice

### Definition
Installation art refers to artworks designed for specific spaces, typically immersive and often interactive. Rather than objects to view, installations transform entire spaces into aesthetic experiences where viewers move through and engage with the work.

### Why It Matters
Installation art expands art's possibilities beyond portable objects to encompassing environments. Understanding installation practice helps you appreciate contemporary art's diversity and immersive potential. Installation art emphasises viewer experience and participation.

### Scenario
When experiencing a large-scale installation artwork, Sade felt immersed rather than observing from distance. The artwork surrounded her, engaged her senses, and required her movement through space. This immersive experience felt fundamentally different from viewing paintings.

### Example
> "James Turrell's light installations create immersive perceptual experiences, transforming how viewers experience space, light, and colour through sophisticated environmental design."

## Digital and New Media Art

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Technology, Interactivity, Virtual space, Contemporary innovation

### Definition
Digital and new media art encompasses artworks created using digital technology: computer-generated images, video art, interactive installations, virtual reality, net art, and digital animation. These forms emerged with technological innovation.

### Why It Matters
Digital and new media art represent contemporary art's frontiers. Understanding these forms prepares you for ongoing technological change in art practice. Digital art raises new questions about interactivity, time-based media, and viewer engagement.

### Scenario
When Zara experienced a virtual reality art installation, she was immersed in a computer-generated world where her movements affected the artwork. This interactive, immersive experience was fundamentally different from traditional artwork. She recognised digital art's unique possibilities.

### Example
> "Refik Anadol creates immersive digital artworks and AI-generated installations that transform architectural spaces into dynamic, ever-changing visual experiences."
