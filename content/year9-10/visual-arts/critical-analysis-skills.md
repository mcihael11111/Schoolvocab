## Formal Analysis

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Visual elements, Composition, Technique, Observation

### Definition
Formal analysis involves objectively describing an artwork's visual properties: colours, shapes, lines, composition, texture, materials, and how visual elements are organised. Formal analysis answers "How is this made?" without interpreting meaning.

### Why It Matters
Formal analysis is foundational to all serious art analysis. Before interpreting meaning, you must observe visual properties accurately. Formal analysis develops precise visual vocabulary and observational skills. Professional art discourse relies on formal analysis.

### Scenario
When learning formal analysis, James forced himself to describe a painting without mentioning meaning: colours (ochre, ultramarine, cadmium), composition (diagonal lines directing eye upward), texture (visible brushstrokes), scale (large monumental form). This discipline revealed visual relationships he hadn't noticed.

### Example
> "Art historians use rigorous formal analysis to understand compositional strategies, colour relationships, and technical choices that create visual meaning."

## Content Analysis

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Subject matter, Symbolism, Iconography, Meaning-making

### Definition
Content analysis examines what an artwork depicts—subject matter and symbolism. It involves identifying what's represented, understanding what symbols mean, recognising cultural or historical references, and interpreting what the subject matter communicates.

### Why It Matters
Understanding content allows you to access artwork's symbolic and narrative dimensions. Content analysis requires cultural and historical knowledge to interpret meaning correctly. Different audiences may interpret content differently based on their knowledge.

### Scenario
When analysing a religious artwork, Sophia recognised biblical references she didn't immediately understand. Research revealed symbolic meanings: specific saints carried identifying attributes, colours carried meanings, compositions referenced theological concepts. Content knowledge transformed her interpretation.

### Example
> "Religious artworks often use complex iconography: halos indicate holiness, specific animals represent saints, particular colours carry theological meanings that require cultural knowledge to interpret."

## Contextual Analysis

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Art history, Cultural context, Historical factors, Socio-political circumstances

### Definition
Contextual analysis examines the circumstances surrounding an artwork's creation: historical moment, cultural values, artist biography, artistic movements, social conditions, and technological possibilities. Context shapes what artists create and what meanings artworks carry.

### Why It Matters
Artworks mean different things in different contexts. Understanding context prevents misinterpretation and enriches appreciation. Contextual knowledge helps you recognise that art doesn't exist in isolation but responds to specific circumstances.

### Scenario
When studying German Expressionist prints, Marcus learned that the movement emerged from World War I trauma, economic devastation, and social upheaval. Understanding this context revealed why the work felt raw, distorted, and emotionally intense. Context explained the style.

### Example
> "Käthe Kollwitz's Expressionist works carrying mourning and suffering emerged from 1920s Germany's post-war trauma and personal losses, with context illuminating meaning."

## Comparative Analysis

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Multiple perspectives, Dialogue, Similarities and differences, Critical thinking

### Definition
Comparative analysis involves examining two or more artworks to identify similarities, differences, and relationships. Comparison might be based on formal properties, subject matter, artist intent, historical context, or conceptual connections.

### Why It Matters
Comparative analysis develops critical thinking by examining how different artists address similar concerns or how the same artist's work evolves. Comparison reveals what's distinctive about individual works and broader patterns across art history.

### Scenario
When comparing portraits by two different artists, Leah recognised that one emphasised realistic likeness while the other prioritised psychological character. This comparison revealed different purposes portraiture could serve and deepened her understanding of both works.

### Example
> "Comparing Renaissance portraits to contemporary self-portraits reveals how artistic priorities have shifted—from recording accurate likeness to exploring identity, representation, and psychological complexity."

## Writing About Art: TEEL Structure

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Writing skills, Art criticism, Academic writing, Argumentation

### Definition
TEEL structure organises art writing: Topic sentence (what you're arguing), Evidence (quotations or visual evidence), Explanation (analysis of evidence), and Link (connection back to thesis). TEEL provides clear structure for building arguments about artwork.

### Why It Matters
TEEL structure organises thinking clearly and helps readers follow your argument. Learning TEEL improves your ability to write persuasively about art. TEEL structure is foundational to academic writing across subjects.

### Scenario
When learning TEEL essay structure, Kai initially wrote vaguely: "The painting is emotional." His teacher asked him to structure arguments: Topic (specify which emotion and how it's created), Evidence (identify specific visual elements creating emotion), Explanation (analyse how these elements work), Link (connect back to overall argument). His writing became clearer and more persuasive.

### Example
> "Art criticism uses TEEL-like structure to build arguments: thesis statement, evidence from artwork or artist statement, analysis explaining evidence, and conclusion reconnecting to thesis."

## Art Terminology and Vocabulary

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Precision, Communication, Art history, Professional discourse

### Definition
Art terminology refers to specialised vocabulary used in art discourse: colour terms (hue, saturation, value), compositional terms (focal point, rule of thirds), technical terms (impasto, sfumato), historical terms (Renaissance, Baroque), and conceptual terms (symbolism, representation).

### Why It Matters
Precise terminology allows clear communication about art. Learning art vocabulary develops your ability to articulate visual experience accurately. Professional artists and art historians use precise terminology—learning it positions you within professional discourse.

### Scenario
When initially discussing artworks, Maya used imprecise language: "That colour is pretty" or "The composition is interesting." Learning to use precise terminology transformed her ability to communicate: "The saturated crimson contrasts with cool blues" or "The off-centre focal point creates dynamic asymmetrical balance."

### Example
> "Art historians use precise terminology to communicate across language barriers—describing 'impasto technique' or 'chiaroscuro effect' precisely communicates visual properties."

## Evidence-Based Interpretation

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Critical thinking, Support, Justification, Rigorous analysis

### Definition
Evidence-based interpretation involves supporting interpretations with specific visual evidence from the artwork. Rather than opinion unsupported by observation, evidence-based interpretation identifies exactly what visual properties support your interpretation.

### Why It Matters
Evidence-based interpretation distinguishes thoughtful art discourse from mere opinion. Learning to support interpretations with evidence develops critical thinking. Professional art writing requires evidence-based argument.

### Scenario
When asked "Why do you interpret this artwork as melancholic?", Nina learned to identify specific evidence: "The cool, muted colour palette, the downward-directed composition, the isolated figure, and the fragmented forms all contribute to melancholic mood." She could justify her interpretation through visual evidence.

### Example
> "Art criticism and analysis support interpretations with specific evidence: 'The red colour symbolises passion because of X, Y, Z visual properties and historical associations.'"

## Multiple Perspectives on Art

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Interpretation, Context, Audience, Validity of different readings

### Definition
Multiple perspectives on art recognises that different viewers, informed by different backgrounds and knowledge, can interpret artworks differently. Multiple valid interpretations can coexist; the same artwork means different things to different people.

### Why It Matters
Understanding multiple perspectives develops intellectual humility and openness. Art doesn't have single correct meanings—your interpretation is valid as long as it's supported by evidence. Understanding this encourages rich, nuanced discourse about art.

### Scenario
When three students analysed the same contemporary artwork, they had different interpretations: one recognised personal symbolism, one saw political critique, one perceived environmental commentary. Their teacher affirmed that all readings were valid if supported by evidence. Diverse perspectives enriched understanding.

### Example
> "Postcolonial art criticism offers perspectives on Western art very different from traditional art history, revealing biases and overlooked meanings in canonical artworks."

## Critical Thinking in Visual Arts

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: Analysis, Questioning, Rigorous engagement, Conceptual depth

### Definition
Critical thinking in visual arts involves questioning assumptions, examining evidence carefully, considering multiple perspectives, recognising bias, and engaging deeply with complex ideas. Critical thinking goes beyond accepting information to actively questioning and analysing.

### Why It Matters
Critical thinking develops rigorous engagement with art beyond surface appreciation. Learning to think critically about art, art history, and artistic value prepares you for thoughtful participation in art culture. Critical thinking resists propaganda and manipulation.

### Scenario
When studying how art history was constructed, Amara recognised that canonical art history had excluded women, non-Western artists, and contemporary artists. Critical thinking revealed that "art history" was one perspective, not objective truth. This awareness transformed her approach to learning.

### Example
> "Critical art historians question whose voices are heard, whose perspectives are marginalised, and how power shapes what art is considered worthy of study and how much it costs."

## Peer Critique and Feedback

- type: apply-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Communication, Collaboration, Growth, Constructive feedback

### Definition
Peer critique and feedback involves receiving and providing thoughtful, constructive feedback on artworks. Effective critique is specific, evidence-based, and balanced between identifying strengths and areas for development.

### Why It Matters
Peer feedback is invaluable for artistic growth. Learning to give and receive critique gracefully prepares you for professional artistic practice. Critique develops communication skills and helps you understand how audiences respond to your work.

### Scenario
When Tania received peer critique, she initially felt defensive. Her classmate specifically identified what was working (strong composition, compelling colour palette) and what needed development (unclear focal point, unresolved background). This balanced, specific feedback was more helpful than generic praise.

### Example
> "Professional artists seek peer feedback, mentor guidance, and curator responses throughout their careers, recognising critique as essential for ongoing development."
