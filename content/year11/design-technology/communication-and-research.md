## Technical Drawing (Orthographic)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: communication and design, CAD, design folio, dimensioning

### Definition
Orthographic projection shows three-dimensional objects as two-dimensional views from multiple directions without perspective distortion. Orthographic drawings include Front Elevation (view looking straight ahead), Side Elevation (view from side), and Plan View (view from above). This system eliminates perspective ambiguity, enabling precise communication where measurements directly correspond to drawing dimensions.

### Why It Matters
Technical drawing is the universal language for manufacturing communication. HSC examiners assess whether your design folio includes appropriate technical drawings enabling someone else to fabricate your design. Orthographic drawings demonstrate professional design communication and show you understand how to translate three-dimensional designs into manufacturing instructions. Additionally, creating orthographic drawings often reveals design conflicts unnoticed in sketch-based thinking.

### Scenario
A student initially communicated a complex component design through perspective sketches that, when examined, proved ambiguous regarding exact angles and relationships. Converting to orthographic projection (front, side and plan views with dimensions) forced clarity, revealing that the sketch design had structural conflicts. This exercise proved orthographic drawing's value for rigorous design thinking.

### Example
> "I created orthographic drawings comprising front elevation (showing height and width dimensions), side elevation (revealing depth), and plan view (showing the footprint). Dimensions specified all critical measurements including: overall dimensions, component positions relative to reference edges, and hole locations. These drawings enabled a colleague to fabricate identical components without ambiguity, demonstrating drawing clarity."

## Technical Drawing (Isometric)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: technical drawing, communication and design, 3D visualisation, CAD

### Definition
Isometric projection shows three-dimensional objects in three dimensions simultaneously (unlike orthographic's multiple views) using isometric axes (120° angles between axes). Lines parallel to these axes maintain their lengths, enabling dimensional accuracy whilst preserving three-dimensional visual clarity. Isometric drawings communicate design intent intuitively whilst retaining technical precision.

### Why It Matters
Isometric drawings bridge the gap between technical orthographic drawings and intuitive visual communication. HSC design folios benefit from both orthographic drawings (for manufacturing communication) and isometric views (for design intent communication). Isometric drawings help design clients and users visualise final products, making them valuable in design presentations. Understanding both orthographic and isometric approaches demonstrates sophisticated communication awareness.

### Scenario
A student's design folio included detailed orthographic drawings but lacked visual communication of the overall design concept. Adding isometric views transformed the folio—viewers could finally visualise how components integrated into a complete product. This experience illustrated how isometric drawings complement technical orthographic communication.

### Example
> "I created isometric drawings showing: overall product assembly (revealing how individual components fit together), component details (demonstrating joint design and structural logic), and exploded views (showing component layering for assembly sequence). Isometric views complemented orthographic drawings by providing intuitive three-dimensional visualisation that orthographic views alone could not convey."

## Technical Drawing (Perspective)

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: technical drawing, communication and design, sketching, visual presentation

### Definition
Perspective drawing mimics human vision by showing objects as the eye sees them—parallel lines converge toward vanishing points, creating depth impression. One-point perspective (single vanishing point), two-point perspective (two vanishing points), and three-point perspective (three vanishing points) create increasing visual complexity. Perspective drawings communicate design aesthetics intuitively but sacrifice precise dimensionality.

### Why It Matters
Perspective drawing serves design presentation purposes rather than manufacturing communication. Perspective views help viewers emotionally connect with designs—a product shown in perspective looks attractive and finished, whereas orthographic views appear technical and abstract. HSC design folios benefit from perspective views communicating design intent to non-technical audiences like users or clients. However, perspective views aren't suitable for manufacturing communication due to dimensional ambiguity caused by perspective distortion.

### Scenario
A student's portfolio included excellent orthographic drawings but appeared purely technical and disconnected from the product's actual aesthetic appeal. Adding colour perspective renderings showing the product in realistic context—a storage solution in an actual bedroom, for example—transformed the presentation. Viewers suddenly understood the design's practical beauty, not just its technical specifications.

### Example
> "I created two-point perspective renderings showing: the product in a realistic living space setting, user interaction with the design (demonstrating how a person would actually use the product), and product detail from multiple viewing angles. Perspective drawings complemented technical orthographic documentation by communicating aesthetic intent and practical usability that technical drawings alone could not convey."

## Sketching and Rendering

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design process, communication and design, ideation, design folio documentation

### Definition
Sketching is rapid hand-drawing used during design development to visualise and explore ideas, test proportions and relationships, and communicate design concepts. Rendering adds visual refinement through colour, shading, tone and detail creating polished design presentations. Sketches range from rough ideation (loose, exploratory) through refined presentation (detailed, finished).

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment includes design folio documentation where sketching demonstrates the iterative design development process. Rather than showing only final designs, including sketches from early ideation through final refinement illustrates your design thinking. Many designs benefit from multiple perspectives communicated through sketches—it's often faster to sketch variations than produce detailed technical drawings of each iteration. HSC examiners value students showing the complete design journey, not just polished endpoints.

### Scenario
A student's design folio contained final technical drawings but no evidence of ideation process. When questioned, she realised she'd completed 30+ sketches exploring variations but discarded them. Reconstructing selected key sketches showing the design evolution (rough initial concepts, then refined iterations, then final design direction) transformed the folio from showing only endpoints to demonstrating sophisticated design thinking.

### Example
> "I documented the design development process through sketched iterations: initial ideation sketches (12 loose explorations of basic form), concept refinement sketches (5 sketches exploring the selected concept with dimensional exploration), proportional studies (3 sketches investigating optimal dimensional relationships), and final presentation rendering (colour perspective showing the resolved design with realistic materials and context). This progression demonstrates the iterative design process underlying the final technical solution."

## Annotation and Dimensioning

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: technical drawing, communication and design, design specifications, manufacturing instructions

### Definition
Annotation and dimensioning add written communication to drawings, specifying measurements, material specifications, surface finishes, assembly instructions and other information essential for manufacturing. Dimensions indicate exact sizes, positioning, tolerances (allowable variation), and reference points. Annotations explain design intent, material choices and assembly procedures.

### Why It Matters
Even beautifully drawn technical drawings remain useless without dimensions—manufacturers cannot fabricate designs without knowing exact measurements. The Major Design Project assessment includes annotated drawings showing you've specified all critical information. Dimensioning also forces design precision; you cannot hide vague ideas behind beautiful drawings when you must explicitly state every measurement. Examiners value students demonstrating this level of design specificity.

### Scenario
A student produced technically excellent orthographic drawings that, when colleagues attempted fabrication, proved incomplete. Critical dimensions were missing (hole sizes not specified, component spacing unclear), making fabrication impossible without constantly asking for clarification. Redesigning the drawings with comprehensive annotation prevented these production failures.

### Example
> "I annotated all technical drawings with: dimensions for every component (specifying tolerances—±0.5mm where critical fit matters, ±1mm where variation acceptable), material specifications (stainless steel 304, hardwood oak, polycarbonate clear), surface finish requirements (sanded to 220-grit, clear varnish, anodised natural finish), and assembly sequence notes (e.g., 'Apply thread-locking compound to fasteners before installation'). Text callouts referenced detail drawings for complex junctions."

## Design Folio Documentation

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design process, communication and design, portfolio development, Major Design Project

### Definition
The design folio (HSC maximum 80 A4 pages) documents the complete design development process, including: design brief and research, evidence of design thinking through sketches and iterations, technical drawings and specifications, prototype development documentation, testing and evaluation results, and final design justification. The folio tells the story of your design from problem identification through resolved solution.

### Why It Matters
The Major Design Project assessment allocates 60% of the mark to the design folio—it's the primary evidence of your design capability. Examiners assess whether your folio demonstrates systematic design thinking, appropriate research, realistic manufacturing planning, and honest evaluation. A poorly organised folio can undermine even a well-executed prototype. Additionally, the folio narrative is as important as individual elements—each page should advance your design story.

### Scenario
A student with an excellent prototype scored poorly on design folio assessment because her documentation was scattered and incomplete—sketches shown without context, technical drawings lacking annotations, and evaluation based on subjective impressions rather than rigorous testing. Rebuilding the folio with clear narrative structure (problem identification → research → development process → prototype → evaluation) transformed the assessment outcome.

### Example
> "My design folio comprised 68 A4 pages organised as: Design Brief & Research (pages 1-10, including needs analysis survey results), Design Development (pages 11-30, showing 25 sketched iterations with written analysis of decision-making), Technical Specifications (pages 31-50, comprehensive orthographic and isometric drawings with detailed annotations), Production Planning (pages 51-60, Gantt chart, cost analysis, WHS considerations), and Evaluation & Outcomes (pages 61-68, testing documentation, user feedback integration, and final reflection on design success)."

## Research Methodologies (Primary)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: needs analysis, design brief, research process, data collection

### Definition
Primary research involves collecting original data directly from sources—interviews (in-depth conversations), surveys (structured questionnaires), observations (watching how people use products), focus groups (group discussions with target users), and testing (measuring product performance). Primary research generates specific data directly addressing your design questions.

### Why It Matters
The NESA syllabus emphasises evidence-based design grounded in authentic understanding of user needs. Primary research demonstrates that you've genuinely investigated your design problem rather than making assumptions. The Major Design Project assessment values evidence of primary research—showing you've interviewed actual target users, observed their real challenges, or surveyed multiple people systematically. This grounding prevents designing solutions to imagined problems that don't actually exist.

### Scenario
A student initially assumed her target users (elderly people) would prefer large buttons on appliance controls based on stereotypes. Conducting interviews with 12 elderly users revealed the real challenge wasn't button size but reaching elevated controls (design assumption was completely wrong). Primary research prevented wasting effort on an unnecessary solution.

### Example
> "I conducted primary research comprising: structured interviews with 8 target users (parents managing children's medications), observing medication administration in real home environments, and surveying 25 parents regarding medication management challenges. Primary research revealed the authentic problem wasn't physical organisation but preventing accidental over-dosing—transforming the design challenge from storage optimisation to safety-focused dispensing control."

## Research Methodologies (Secondary)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design brief, research process, literature review, contextual understanding

### Definition
Secondary research involves analysing existing information—published studies, competitor product analysis, design history, market reports, and expert publications. Secondary research provides context, identifies existing solutions, reveals industry best practices, and builds foundational understanding without requiring original data collection.

### Why It Matters
Secondary research informs informed design—you're not starting from complete ignorance but building on existing knowledge. The Major Design Project assessment values students demonstrating awareness of existing solutions and design context. Secondary research prevents reinventing wheels that already exist or making mistakes others have already learned from. Additionally, secondary research enables comparative analysis—understanding how existing solutions approach similar problems informs your own design decisions.

### Scenario
A student designing a new kitchen storage solution conducted secondary research analysing 15 existing products (identifying common features, common problems, design trends), reviewed academic ergonomics literature (discovering human reach guidelines), and studied design history (understanding how kitchen organisation has evolved). This contextual foundation prevented naïve design mistakes and positioned their solution within design tradition.

### Example
> "I conducted extensive secondary research including: competitor product analysis (examining 12 similar storage systems identifying strengths and limitations), published anthropometric data (human reach and manipulation standards), ergonomic design literature, and market trend analysis. Secondary research revealed that accessibility for mobility-impaired users remained an underexplored opportunity—informing my design focus on universal design principles."

## Survey and Questionnaire Design

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: primary research, needs analysis, data collection, quantitative research

### Definition
Survey design involves creating structured questionnaires systematically gathering quantitative data from multiple respondents. Effective surveys employ clear questions avoiding ambiguity, appropriate response options (yes/no, rating scales, multiple choice), and sufficient participants for statistical validity. Survey design methodology directly influences data quality—poorly designed surveys generate unreliable data regardless of sample size.

### Why It Matters
Surveys are accessible primary research methods suitable for HSC projects—you can survey dozens of people relatively quickly and systematically analyse responses. However, survey design requires discipline; casual questionnaires produce unreliable data. The Major Design Project assessment values students demonstrating research rigour—including pilot-testing surveys with sample users, excluding ambiguous questions, and analysing results systematically rather than cherry-picking convenient data.

### Scenario
A student's initial survey asked "Would you like a better kitchen organiser?"—almost everyone answered yes, proving nothing. Redesigned surveys asked specific questions: "How much time daily searching for kitchen items?" (quantifying the actual problem), "Which existing storage solutions have you tried?" (identifying competitor awareness), and "What features matter most?" (enabling priority ranking). This improved survey revealed actual user priorities rather than vague affirmations.

### Example
> "I designed a survey comprising: demographic questions (age, household size, kitchen type), quantitative usage questions (time spent searching daily, frequency of organisation frustration on 1-10 scales), preference questions (rating importance of accessibility, visibility, cost, aesthetics on 1-5 scales), and open-ended questions capturing user descriptions. I pilot-tested the survey with 3 users (identifying confusing questions), revised based on feedback, then surveyed 27 target users systematically, producing data enabling quantified priority ranking."

## Data Analysis for Design

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: research methodologies, needs analysis, survey design, decision-making

### Definition
Data analysis transforms raw research information into actionable insights supporting design decisions. Analysis methods include quantitative analysis (calculating percentages, averages, identifying patterns) and qualitative analysis (identifying themes, understanding meanings, recognising connections). Systematic analysis prevents cherry-picking convenient data and reveals authentic patterns.

### Why It Matters
Collecting data means nothing without rigorous analysis transforming it into design guidance. The Major Design Project assessment values students demonstrating analytical thinking—you can't simply state "survey respondents said X" without analysing what X means for design. Did 60% mention X, making it a significant trend, or 20%, making it a minority view? Systematic analysis prevents overweighting unrepresentative feedback and ensures design decisions reflect actual data patterns rather than convenient anecdotes.

### Scenario
A student surveyed users about preferred storage configurations, receiving diverse responses. Rather than pursuing whatever first responses suggested, she systematically analysed data: 73% prioritised accessibility, 68% valued visibility, 42% mentioned cost concerns. This priority ranking (derived from actual data) guided design focus to accessibility and visibility first, cost second. Data analysis prevented pursuing the wrong priorities.

### Example
> "I analysed survey responses systematically: accessibility priority ranked first (mentioned by 73% of respondents, rated average 4.6/5 importance), visibility second (68% mentioned, 4.4/5 importance rating), cost third (42% mentioned, 3.2/5 importance), and aesthetics fourth (36% mentioned, 2.8/5 importance). Analysing response frequency and rating scores together identified consensus priorities rather than allowing minority responses to dominate decision-making."

## Presentation Techniques

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: communication and design, design folio, audience engagement, visual communication

### Definition
Presentation techniques encompass methods for communicating design ideas effectively to audiences—clear layout, visual hierarchy (emphasising important information), effective colour use (supporting understanding), consistent formatting, appropriate typography, and integration of images that enhance rather than distract. Good presentation makes complex information accessible; poor presentation obscures clear ideas.

### Why It Matters
Your design folio and presentation ability matter as much as the actual design—no matter how excellent your design is, poor presentation undermines its impact. HSC examiners assess whether your design folio communicates effectively. Presentation techniques aren't superficial decoration but essential communication tools. Additionally, the real design industry relies heavily on presentation skills—designers must persuade clients, stakeholders and manufacturers of design merit.

### Scenario
A student's design folio contained excellent work but suffered poor presentation: inconsistent page layouts, clashing colours, typography that contradicted organisation logic, and excessive text overwhelming visuals. Redesigning the presentation (consistent visual hierarchy, coherent colour scheme, supporting text rather than dominating it, strategic whitespace) transformed the same underlying content into a compelling document.

### Example
> "I designed the folio layout with: consistent two-column grid organising information logically, limited colour palette (neutral backgrounds, accent colours highlighting key sections), clear typography hierarchy (large headings establishing sections, body text legible but secondary), and integration of visuals (photographs and drawings occupying 40% of page space) with supporting text. This presentation structure enabled readers to navigate the 68-page folio effectively, finding information intuitively."

## Digital Communication Tools

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: presentation techniques, design folio, CAD, design documentation

### Definition
Digital communication tools encompass software enabling design documentation and presentation—page layout software (Adobe InDesign, Affinity Publisher, Canva) for folio creation, CAD software (AutoCAD, Fusion 360) for technical drawing, image editing software (Photoshop, GIMP) for rendering preparation, and presentation software (PowerPoint, Keynote) for design pitches. Each tool suits specific communication purposes.

### Why It Matters
Contemporary design relies on digital tools for professional communication. Demonstrating competence with appropriate tools (not just word processors for technical documentation) signals professional understanding. The HSC assessment values design folios appearing professionally formatted using appropriate design tools. However, sophistication isn't about using the most complex software; it's about selecting tools matching communication purposes and using them effectively.

### Scenario
A student created her design folio using a basic word processor, struggling with layout control and visual design. Switching to dedicated page layout software (which her school provided) enabled professional-quality design folio production—consistent formatting, controlled typography, sophisticated visual hierarchy. The same content appeared dramatically more professional in proper design software.

### Example
> "I created the design folio using Affinity Publisher (professional page layout software) enabling: precise grid-based layout consistency, sophisticated typography with controlled kerning and leading, integration of CAD drawings and photographs with captions, and efficient management of 68-page document structure. I produced technical drawings using Fusion 360 CAD (exporting to PDF for folio inclusion), enabling parametric design revision and precision dimensioning impossible in hand drawing."

## Portfolio Layout and Design

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: presentation techniques, design folio, communication and design, professional presentation

### Definition
Portfolio layout encompasses the overall visual organisation of design documentation—page grid systems (organising content spatially), colour schemes (supporting hierarchy and mood), typography systems (consistent font usage across hierarchy levels), image integration (appropriate sizing and captioning), and navigation systems (enabling readers to find information). Strong portfolio layout makes complex information accessible and visually cohesive.

### Why It Matters
Your design folio is itself a design project—the same principles governing your product design apply to folio design. HSC examiners assess folio presentation as evidence of design thinking; a poorly designed folio suggests poor design thinking overall. Additionally, designing your folio teaches practical application of design principles you're studying. The effort invested in folio design demonstrates seriousness about your project and respect for your audience.

### Scenario
A student's design folio employed different page layouts throughout—inconsistent grid systems, varying margins, different heading styles on each page. This visual chaos made the folio confusing to navigate despite excellent underlying work. Redesigning with consistent page layouts (uniform grid, consistent margins, unified typography) transformed chaos into cohesion, making the work dramatically more effective.

### Example
> "I designed the folio using a consistent two-column layout grid: left column (narrower, ~3cm) for titles and annotations, right column (wider, ~14cm) for images and primary content. Consistent 2cm margins throughout, unified typography (Helvetica Neue for headings, Garamond for body text), limited colour palette (white background, navy blue accents), and strategic whitespace created visual cohesion across 68 pages. This systematic approach enabled readers to navigate intuitively, focusing on content rather than struggling with presentation inconsistency."
