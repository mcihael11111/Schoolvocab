## Design Process

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: design brief, needs analysis, design specifications

### Definition
The design process is a systematic approach comprising five distinct phases: identify (recognising a problem or need), research (gathering information), generate (creating initial ideas), develop (refining selected concepts) and evaluate (assessing against criteria). This iterative method ensures design solutions address authentic human needs through structured methodologies rather than random problem-solving.

### Why It Matters
The HSC Major Design Project requires you to demonstrate competence in each phase. Examiners assess not just your final product but how systematically you progressed through these stages. Understanding the design process framework allows you to justify decisions in your design folio and articulate your thinking in the written examination.

### Scenario
A Year 11 student recognised that their elderly neighbour struggled to reach high cupboards in the kitchen. Rather than jumping to solutions, they spent time identifying the exact problem, researching ergonomic standards and existing solutions, generating multiple ideas through sketching, developing the most feasible option through prototyping, and finally evaluating their prototype against success criteria. This structured approach proved far more effective than their initial impulse to simply modify a step ladder.

### Example
> "In the identify phase, I discovered that my target users (primary school teachers) wasted 20 minutes daily searching for lesson resources. This genuine need drove all subsequent design decisions throughout my project."

## Design Brief Writing

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: identify phase, needs analysis, target market, design specifications

### Definition
A design brief is a clearly written statement that defines a design problem, including the purpose, constraints, user needs and success criteria. It functions as a contract between the designer and the client, ensuring both parties understand what the final design solution should achieve and within what parameters it must operate.

### Why It Matters
The NESA syllabus explicitly requires a clearly defined design brief as the starting point of your Major Design Project. Assessors examine whether your brief articulates a genuine need and identifies specific users. A weak brief often leads to unfocused design work; a strong brief provides direction for all subsequent decisions and allows you to evaluate whether you successfully solved the stated problem.

### Scenario
A student initially wrote: "Design a bag." After teacher feedback, they rewrote it as: "Design a lightweight, durable backpack for Year 7 students that safely carries 5kg of textbooks, fits within 25L capacity, costs under $80 to produce, and encourages independent organisation through internal compartmentalisation." This specific brief transformed their entire project, enabling measurable evaluation.

### Example
> "Design a reusable food container system for busy office workers (aged 25-45) that eliminates single-use plastic lunch containers, maintains food temperature for 6 hours, costs less than $25, and encourages sustainable habits through visual design."

## Needs Analysis

- type: do-it
- level: beginner
- related: design brief, target market, research methodologies, survey design

### Definition
Needs analysis is systematic investigation into the genuine requirements of a target user group, identifying what they truly need (not what they think they want). It employs research methods including surveys, interviews, observations and contextual enquiry to establish authentic design problems before proposing solutions.

### Why It Matters
Many design failures occur because designers misidentify or misunderstand actual user needs. The HSC assessment criteria specifically value evidence of genuine research into your target users' requirements. Conducting rigorous needs analysis demonstrates critical thinking and ensures your design solution addresses real problems, which strengthens both your Major Design Project and responses in the written examination.

### Scenario
A student planning to design a drink bottle for gym-goers conducted interviews with 15 regular gym users rather than assuming what they needed. She discovered that temperature retention ranked below ease of cleaning—contradicting her initial assumptions. This insight redirected her entire design focus, demonstrating how needs analysis prevents wasted effort.

### Example
> "My survey of 24 secondary students revealed that 87% found existing pencil cases impractical because items scatter when opened, 79% wanted transparent compartments to locate items quickly, and all respondents used mechanical pencils regularly despite most cases lacking suitable holders."

## Target Market

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: needs analysis, design brief, user profile, market research

### Definition
Target market refers to the specific group of people for whom a design is intended, defined by demographic characteristics (age, gender, income), psychographic factors (lifestyle, values, interests) and behavioural patterns. Clear target market definition enables focused design decisions because products are designed for specific people rather than vague generalities.

### Why It Matters
Examiners assess whether your design decisions reflect genuine understanding of your target users' characteristics and preferences. Vague target markets (e.g., "teenagers") produce unfocused design work. Specific market definition (e.g., "eco-conscious females aged 18-25 in metropolitan areas earning $25,000-50,000 annually") enables you to justify material choices, aesthetic decisions and functionality in your design folio and written responses.

### Scenario
Two students both designed storage solutions but with different target markets. Student A designed for "busy people" producing a generic organiser. Student B identified "shift-working nurses with 1-2 children at home" and designed a system accommodating irregular schedules and safety requirements specific to this group. Student B's focused approach produced more coherent and justifiable design decisions.

### Example
> "My target market comprises females aged 35-50 in professional careers, earning $70,000+, environmentally conscious, with limited time for household tasks, who value durability and aesthetic appeal equally, and actively purchase premium sustainable products despite higher cost."

## Design Specifications

- type: do-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design brief, constraints, needs analysis, evaluation criteria

### Definition
Design specifications are detailed, measurable requirements that a final design solution must meet, typically expressed numerically or using clear parameters. Specifications translate the design brief into objective criteria against which a finished design can be evaluated, covering dimensions, materials, performance standards, safety requirements and aesthetic guidelines.

### Why It Matters
Design specifications separate subjective opinions from objective evaluation. The HSC Major Design Project assessment requires you to evaluate your final work against predetermined criteria. Clear specifications enable you to demonstrate that your solution definitively met or exceeded requirements, providing evidence of successful design. Additionally, manufacturers and clients rely on specifications to assess design feasibility.

### Scenario
A student designing a desk organiser wrote general specs: "strong", "attractive", "practical". After guidance, they specified: maximum dimensions 300mm × 200mm × 150mm, must support 2kg distributed load across three compartments, accommodate items 150mm-15mm in length, cost under $30 to produce, use sustainable materials, and feature a minimalist aesthetic compatible with modern office environments. These specifications enabled precise evaluation.

### Example
> "Weight capacity: minimum 5kg distributed load; Dimensions: 450mm H × 380mm W × 280mm D; Materials: food-grade silicone (main), anodised aluminium (frame); Temperature tolerance: -20°C to +60°C; Cost per unit: maximum $12; Cleaning method: dishwasher safe; Aesthetic: contemporary minimalist style compatible with kitchen decor."

## Constraints and Considerations

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design specifications, design brief, feasibility, ethical design

### Definition
Constraints are limitations that restrict design possibilities, including physical limitations (material properties, manufacturing capabilities), economic constraints (budget, production costs), technical constraints (performance requirements) and regulatory constraints (safety standards, legal requirements). Considerations extend beyond constraints to encompass broader factors affecting design decisions.

### Why It Matters
Real-world design occurs within multiple constraints; unrealistic designs fail when manufactured or used. The HSC examination assesses your understanding that excellent designers creatively work within constraints rather than ignoring them. Acknowledging constraints in your design folio demonstrates maturity and practical knowledge. Furthermore, identifying constraints early prevents designing solutions that cannot feasibly be produced or used safely.

### Scenario
A student designed a lightweight food container but ignored manufacturing constraints. When developing a prototype, they discovered injection moulding required wall thickness of minimum 2mm, making their thin-walled design impossible. Learning this late in their project forced costly redesign. A peer who investigated manufacturing constraints early designed within feasible parameters from the start.

### Example
> "Primary constraints include: budget limitation of $400 for prototype production, manufacturing equipment restricted to laser cutting and hand assembly, timeframe of 12 weeks for development, target users' physical abilities (users aged 65+ with reduced hand strength), and safety standards requiring edge radii exceeding 3mm and material toxicity certification."

## Aesthetics in Design

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design movements, cultural influences, visual principles, target market

### Definition
Aesthetics encompasses the visual and sensory qualities that make a design appealing, including colour, form, texture, proportion and overall visual harmony. Aesthetic choices communicate meaning, reflect cultural values, influence user perception and significantly impact whether users accept or reject a design, independent of functional performance.

### Why It Matters
HSC assessors examine whether aesthetic decisions are purposeful and justified, not arbitrary. Understanding aesthetics enables you to articulate why specific colours, forms or textures suit your target market and design intent. This is particularly important in the written examination where you must analyse how artists and designers use aesthetic choices to communicate meaning. Aesthetics directly influence whether users embrace or abandon otherwise functional designs.

### Scenario
Two students designed phone stands with identical functionality. Student A chose bright neon colours with sharp angular forms—visually jarring and rejected by their target market of corporate professionals seeking understated desk accessories. Student B selected natural wood with curved forms, creating aesthetic cohesion with professional office environments. Identical function, but aesthetics determined user acceptance.

### Example
> "I selected a muted sage-green colour palette reflecting contemporary interior design trends and environmental consciousness aligning with my target market's values. Curved forms convey approachability and comfort, contrasting with harsh industrial aesthetics that might alienate users seeking a nurturing workspace design."

## Ergonomics

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: anthropometrics, target market, design specifications, functionality

### Definition
Ergonomics is the science of designing products and environments to fit human needs, capabilities and limitations, encompassing how humans physically interact with objects. Ergonomic design considers factors including comfort, safety, efficiency of movement and prevention of strain or injury when using products over extended periods.

### Why It Matters
Products that ignore ergonomic principles create user discomfort, injury or rejection despite otherwise sound design. The HSC syllabus requires understanding that human-centred design prioritises user wellbeing. Demonstrating ergonomic knowledge in your Major Design Project—through decisions about handle shape, seat height, visual display positioning—shows sophisticated design thinking. Ergonomic failures also appear frequently in HSC case study analysis, requiring students to evaluate designs critically.

### Scenario
A student designed a children's step stool for accessing high shelves. Initial designs featured smooth cylindrical handles and a single height. After researching ergonomics, they developed handles with anatomically-shaped grips reducing hand strain, added a non-slip surface accommodating different hand sizes, and incorporated two height options for varying age groups. These ergonomic considerations transformed a basic product into a genuinely useful design.

### Example
> "The handle diameter of 35mm accommodates hand sizes from 5th to 95th percentile, reducing grip strain during extended use. Backrest angle of 105° supports natural spine curvature and reduces lower back strain for users seated longer than 30 minutes. Non-slip tread pattern prevents slipping on wet surfaces, addressing safety concerns for elderly users with reduced proprioception."

## Anthropometrics

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: ergonomics, target market, design specifications, universal design

### Definition
Anthropometrics is the scientific measurement of human body dimensions and proportions. It provides statistical data about human size variation across age groups, genders and populations, enabling designers to create products that accommodate diverse users rather than designing for a single "average" person who doesn't actually exist.

### Why It Matters
Anthropometric data transforms design from guesswork into evidence-based practice. The HSC assessment criteria value designers who consider user diversity rather than designing for imagined average users. Understanding percentile ranges (e.g., designing to accommodate 5th to 95th percentile) ensures products serve broad user populations. This is essential for accessible design and appears frequently in HSC questions about inclusive design and design for disability.

### Scenario
A student designed a workspace desk without consulting anthropometric data, setting height at 750mm to suit her own proportions. When testing with users ranging from 150cm to 195cm tall, most found the desk uncomfortable. Redesign using anthropometric data specifying desk height accommodation from 5th percentile female to 95th percentile male heights created a product truly serving diverse users.

### Example
> "Handlebar height adjusts between 850mm and 1050mm accommodating users from 150th percentile female (approximately 155cm tall) to 95th percentile male (approximately 188cm tall). Reach envelope extends 600-800mm from the user's body, based on anthropometric data for arm reach and preventing upper back strain during extended use periods."

## Functionality

- type: know-it
- level: beginner
- related: design specifications, design brief, evaluation, performance testing

### Definition
Functionality refers to how well a design performs its intended purpose and meets stated requirements. A functional design works reliably, achieves its objectives efficiently and solves the problem it was designed to address. Functionality is often distinguished from aesthetics, though excellent design integrates both seamlessly.

### Why It Matters
A design may look beautiful but fail if it doesn't function. The HSC syllabus emphasises that design solves problems; purely decorative solutions without functional improvement represent design failure. When evaluating your Major Design Project, examiners assess whether your solution genuinely functions as intended. In the written examination, you must distinguish between aesthetically pleasing designs and functionally effective designs, recognising that the best designs excel at both.

### Scenario
A student's first prototype for a study organiser featured attractive compartments but failed when users tried stacking books—compartments collapsed under load. The design looked good but didn't function. Only after structural testing and reinforcement did the design become truly functional, demonstrating that aesthetics alone cannot substitute for engineering integrity.

### Example
> "My storage system must reliably hold 8kg of textbooks without structural deformation, accommodate items ranging from small pens to A4 notebooks within designated sections, remain stable when moved or tilted, and withstand daily use for minimum two years without fastener loosening or joint failure."

## Innovation vs Invention

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design movements, innovation case study, intellectual property, entrepreneurship

### Definition
Invention creates something entirely new that didn't previously exist (e.g., the first smartphone). Innovation improves upon existing designs or applies existing ideas in new contexts (e.g., improved smartphone interfaces). Most contemporary design is innovative rather than inventive, as creating genuinely novel solutions is rare; most design excellence comes from refining existing concepts.

### Why It Matters
The HSC written examination includes an innovation case study question requiring you to distinguish between innovation and invention. Understanding this distinction prevents overestimating the novelty of your own designs. Rather than seeking unprecedented invention, HSC assessment values thoughtful innovation—improving existing solutions to better meet user needs or implementing creative combinations of existing ideas. This more realistic understanding of design prevents discouragement and produces authentic design outcomes.

### Scenario
A student designing reusable lunch containers thought their design needed to be completely original to be worthy. After studying the innovation/invention distinction, they recognised their strength was improving containers through innovative compartmentalisation inspired by Japanese bento boxes combined with modern sustainable materials. This repositioning allowed them to focus on genuine improvements rather than impossible originality.

### Example
> "Rather than inventing entirely new technology, my design innovatively combines established features: thermochromic paint indicating temperature (existing technology from medicine), modular compartment design (established organisational principle), and recycled ocean plastic (existing sustainable material). This innovative integration creates improved functionality for busy professionals without requiring technological invention."

## Design Movements (Art Deco)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design history, aesthetics, cultural influences, Bauhaus, Modernism

### Definition
Art Deco, flourishing in the 1920s-1930s, emphasised geometric forms, luxury materials, bold colours and streamlined shapes inspired by technology and speed. It represented optimism about industrial progress whilst maintaining decorative richness, creating instantly recognisable visual style through symmetry, zigzag patterns and stylised natural forms.

### Why It Matters
Art Deco exemplifies how design movements reflect historical periods and cultural values. Understanding Art Deco—its emphasis on geometric precision, luxury craftsmanship and celebration of modernity—helps you analyse historical designs in the HSC examination and recognise how contemporary design continues borrowing from established movements. Studying design movements develops critical thinking about design as cultural commentary, essential for extended response questions.

### Scenario
A student analysing Art Deco building facades recognised the geometric patterns, metallic accents and streamlined forms as reflecting 1920s fascination with technology and mechanisation. This contextual understanding enabled sophisticated analysis of how design choices communicated cultural values, demonstrating the kind of critical thinking HSC examiners reward.

### Example
> "Art Deco design emphasises geometric precision visible in the Chrysler Building's triangular window patterns and stepped crown, streamlined forms suggesting speed and technological progress, and luxurious materials including stainless steel and terrazzo. These choices celebrated industrial modernity whilst maintaining handcrafted decoration, reflecting 1920s optimism about technology."

## Design Movements (Bauhaus)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: design movements, Modernism, design theory, Art Deco, Memphis

### Definition
Bauhaus, a German design school operating 1919-1933, integrated craftsmanship with industrial production through principles of simplicity, functionality and elimination of unnecessary ornament. It emphasised that good design should be accessible to ordinary people through democratic mass production rather than luxury craftsmanship, fundamentally reshaping modern design philosophy.

### Why It Matters
Bauhaus principles underpin much contemporary design philosophy—the belief that functional design serves users better than decoration. Understanding Bauhaus helps you articulate why minimalist, functional designs matter. In HSC analysis, recognising Bauhaus influence in modern product design demonstrates sophisticated understanding of design history's ongoing impact. Many design case studies feature Bauhaus-influenced products, requiring students to explain these principles.

### Scenario
A student analysing modern kitchen products recognised Bauhaus principles in minimalist handles, integrated storage and elimination of decorative elements. Understanding that these choices reflected the "form follows function" philosophy enabled analysis beyond surface observation, explaining designer intent and demonstrating how 1920s ideas continue influencing contemporary design.

### Example
> "Bauhaus design prioritises functionality over decoration through the maxim 'form follows function.' The iconic Barcelona Chair exemplifies this approach: its steel frame construction uses industrial materials democratically available for mass production, its geometric form provides comfort through mathematical proportion rather than upholstered decoration, and its elegant simplicity reflects the belief that good design serves human needs without unnecessary ornament."

## Design Movements (Modernism)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Bauhaus, design history, design theory, Innovation case study

### Definition
Modernism, spanning much of the twentieth century, rejected historical styles and ornamentation in favour of designs reflecting contemporary materials and manufacturing technologies. Modernism emphasised clean lines, geometric forms, functional purity and the belief that design should express its age—producing timeless solutions through essential reduction rather than stylistic decoration.

### Why It Matters
Modernism directly influences contemporary design philosophy, making it essential for HSC analysis. Many acclaimed designers studied by HSC students—including Charles and Ray Eames—worked within modernist frameworks. Understanding modernism's core principles helps you evaluate contemporary design critically, recognising which choices derive from functional necessity versus which represent stylistic preferences. This distinction appears frequently in HSC extended response questions.

### Scenario
A student analysing modern furniture recognised modernist principles in Eames Lounge Chair design: the plywood shell's form derives from wood's structural properties and manufacturing techniques, leather upholstery integrates comfort through essential means rather than decorative luxury, and the design's minimal ornamentation allows material beauty to dominate. This analysis demonstrated understanding modernism beyond memorised facts.

### Example
> "Modernist design philosophy asserts that authentic design emerges from honest use of materials and contemporary manufacturing methods. The Egg Chair's form reflects how a single sheet of fibreglass can be moulded, its minimal colour variation celebrates material itself, and its organic shape provides comfort through structural form rather than added cushioning—exemplifying the modernist principle that design's beauty derives from functional essence."

## Design Movements (Memphis)

- type: know-it
- level: intermediate
- related: Postmodernism, design history, aesthetics, cultural design

### Definition
Memphis design, emerging in 1980s Italy, deliberately rejected modernism's minimalism and "good taste" through playful colour combinations, irregular forms, historical pastiche and kitsch references. It questioned the notion of "correct" design, celebrating plurality, individuality and expressive form over functional purity, treating design as cultural commentary and artistic expression.

### Why It Matters
Memphis represents a radical philosophical shift where designers rejected established "rules" about what constitutes good design. For HSC students, studying Memphis develops understanding that design reflects cultural values and philosophical positions rather than representing universal truths. The movement's explicit rejection of modernism's authority helps you critically evaluate design claims and recognise that contemporary design encompasses multiple valid approaches. Memphis also appears in postmodern design analysis questions.

### Scenario
A student initially thought Memphis design looked "wrong"—garish colours, awkward proportions, clashing patterns. Understanding that these choices deliberately challenged modernist authority transformed their perspective. They recognised Memphis design as cultural commentary rejecting elitism in design taste, enabling sophisticated analysis of design as philosophy rather than just aesthetics.

### Example
> "Memphis design deliberately rejected modernism's minimalist 'good taste' through startling colour combinations (hot pink with electric yellow), irregular geometric forms challenging functional logic, and historical pastiche referencing various styles simultaneously. The Carlton Bookcase exemplifies this approach with its asymmetrical form, laminated plastic surfaces featuring clashing patterns, and colours producing visual tension—all chosen specifically to challenge the notion of tasteful, correct design."

## Design for Disability/Inclusivity

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: universal design, anthropometrics, ergonomics, ethical design, target market

### Definition
Designing for disability and inclusivity means creating products and systems usable by people with diverse abilities, including people with physical, sensory or cognitive disabilities. Rather than designing for "average" able-bodied users, inclusive design recognises human diversity as normal and develops solutions accommodating this full spectrum of human variation.

### Why It Matters
The NESA syllabus explicitly addresses design for disability as part of ethical design practice. HSC assessment rewards designs demonstrating genuine consideration of diverse users. Inclusive design isn't a specialised niche but standard professional practice that benefits all users—ramps help wheelchair users, parents with strollers, and people carrying heavy items. Understanding inclusive design demonstrates the kind of human-centred, ethical thinking examiners value. Additionally, Australia's Disability Discrimination Act requires consideration of accessibility in many contexts.

### Scenario
A student initially designed a voting system interface with small buttons requiring precise finger dexterity and colour-coded options relying on colour vision. After considering disabled users, they redesigned with larger buttons, tactile feedback, high-contrast black/white interface, voice prompts, and simplified navigation. This inclusive redesign improved usability for elderly voters, people with arthritis and people with vision impairments—far more users than the disabled population alone.

### Example
> "My door handle design incorporates: lever-style handle (usable without gripping strength required by traditional knobs), high contrast against door frame (assisting users with low vision), 35mm diameter accommodating smaller hands, textured surface providing tactile feedback for blind users, and positioning at 1000mm height accommodating wheelchair users. These features ensure accessibility without requiring specialised 'disabled' versions."

## Planned Obsolescence

- type: think-about-it
- level: intermediate
- related: ethical design, sustainability, design for society, environmental impact

### Definition
Planned obsolescence describes the deliberate design of products with limited functional lifespans, ensuring consumers must replace items regularly even though they could function longer. This occurs through material degradation (cheap components failing predictably), technological obsolescence (incompatible new systems), or aesthetic obsolescence (fashion-driven replacement despite continued functionality).

### Why It Matters
Planned obsolescence contradicts sustainable design principles and raises significant ethical questions—issues explicitly addressed in the HSC syllabus. Understanding planned obsolescence enables you to critique designs critically in examination questions, recognising how manufacturers' financial interests sometimes conflict with environmental and consumer wellbeing. This demonstrates the sophisticated ethical thinking required for top HSC bands. Additionally, your own design should actively resist planned obsolescence, demonstrating commitment to sustainable practice.

### Scenario
A student analysing smartphone design recognised deliberate choices reducing longevity: non-replaceable batteries degrading after 2-3 years, proprietary connectors preventing affordable repairs, and software updates slowing older devices. Understanding these were deliberate business decisions (not inevitable technology) enabled ethical critique: this design prioritises manufacturer profit over environmental responsibility and consumer value.

### Example
> "Fast fashion clothing exemplifies planned obsolescence through aesthetic obsolescence (designs deliberately dated through trendy cuts ensuring consumers feel pressure to replace items regardless of physical condition) and material obsolescence (fabrics weakening predictably to discourage long-term use). This contrasts with sustainable design philosophy emphasising durability, timelessness and extended product lifespan as fundamental responsibilities."

## Ethical Design

- type: think-about-it
- level: advanced
- related: design for disability, sustainability, design for society, planned obsolescence

### Definition
Ethical design prioritises human wellbeing and social responsibility above profit maximisation, considering impacts on users, communities and environment. Ethical designers ask not just "can we design this?" and "will consumers buy this?" but "should we design this?" and "what are the broader consequences of our design decisions?"

### Why It Matters
The HSC Design and Technology syllabus identifies ethical design as a key content area. Examiners assess whether students recognise design's broader social and environmental responsibilities. In your Major Design Project, demonstrating ethical thinking—through sustainable material choices, accessibility considerations, honest communication, fair manufacturing practices—elevates your work from technically competent to genuinely excellent. Written examination questions frequently explore design's ethical dimensions, requiring students to move beyond surface analysis to deeper moral reasoning.

### Scenario
A student designing a fashion brand initially planned using cheap overseas labour to maximise profit margins. Recognising this contradicted ethical design principles, they researched fair-trade manufacturing, developed transparent supply chain documentation, and accepted smaller profit margins to ensure workers received living wages and safe conditions. This ethical approach generated stronger customer loyalty than the cheaper alternative would have produced.

### Example
> "Ethical design requires transparency about environmental impact, honest material claims without greenwashing, consideration of labour conditions throughout supply chains, accessibility for diverse users regardless of profitability, and long-term thinking about environmental legacy. My design choices reflect these commitments: sustainably sourced materials with transparent certification, fair-trade manufacturing with published worker wage data, designed for 15-year lifespan rather than disposability, and accessibility features increasing production costs but serving users authentically."
